How to File a CFPB Complaint Against Your Student Loan Servicer: The November 2026 Template That Forces a Written Response in 60 Days After the 33-Lawmaker McMahon Letter

On November 5, 2026 thirty-three members of Congress sent Secretary of Education Linda McMahon a letter demanding action on documented student loan servicing errors flagged by eight state Student Loan Ombudspersons. The letter cites a measurable spike in CFPB complaint volume against MOHELA, Nelnet, Aidvantage, and EdFinancial since collections restarted on October 1, 2026. If your servicer has mishandled a RAP payment, miscounted PSLF months, reversed the interest waiver, or failed to enroll you in autopay, a properly filed CFPB complaint forces a written servicer response within 60 days and creates a federal paper trail your servicer cannot ignore. Here is the exact submission flow, the four pieces of evidence to attach, the sample complaint language that gets results, and the escalation ladder when the servicer response is non-responsive.

Complaint volume at the CFPB against the four largest federal student loan servicers has roughly tripled from pre-restart baselines. MOHELA, Nelnet, Aidvantage, and EdFinancial collectively received about 18,000 student loan complaints in the 12 months ending September 2026, concentrated in the October restart window when first bills generated from freshly transitioned accounts. The 33-lawmaker letter to Secretary McMahon names four complaint categories as federally significant: RAP payment calculation errors, PSLF qualifying-payment count disputes, interest waiver reversals on accounts auto-enrolled from SAVE, and autopay enrollment failures that have cost borrowers the 0.25 percent autopay interest reduction and, in some cases, the 1 percent extended-autopay discount that briefly ran through September 30, 2026.

The political pressure matters, but it does not fix your individual servicing error. The CFPB complaint does. The complaint process is free, takes about 20 minutes to file, requires no attorney, and triggers a federally mandated 60-day servicer response timeline. The complaint record also enters the CFPB's public complaint database, which is directly reviewed by congressional staff drafting letters like the one McMahon received this week. Each individual complaint is a data point that compounds into enforcement pressure; your complaint helps both your own account and every other borrower hitting the same error.

The Four Servicing Error Categories the November 5 Letter Names

The 33-lawmaker letter cites error categories the eight state Student Loan Ombudspersons flagged repeatedly through August, September, and October 2026. Each one maps to a CFPB complaint sub-category:

RAP payment amount wrong. The RAP monthly payment is calculated as 1 percent to 10 percent of adjusted gross income divided by 12, tiered by income band, with a $50-per-dependent deduction applied after the tier calculation. Documented errors include servicers applying the dependent deduction before the tier calculation (which produces a higher payment), omitting the deduction entirely, using the wrong AGI year, and applying the married-filing-jointly combined AGI to a married-filing-separately borrower. Model your correct payment at the RAP Calculator and compare against what your servicer is billing.

PSLF count wrong after SAVE-to-RAP transition. Borrowers who were enrolled in SAVE before its 2026 phase-out and were auto-enrolled into RAP in July or August should have retained every qualifying PSLF month earned on SAVE, including the SAVE forbearance months from the 2024 to 2025 litigation period, which were treated as qualifying time for most borrowers under the IDR Account Adjustment carryover. Documented errors include missing SAVE forbearance months, missing employer certification months, and the carryover count being reset to zero on servicer transfer. Verify against the PSLF Tracker model.

Interest waiver reversal. RAP's 50 percent interest match provision waives half of any unpaid monthly interest; the SAVE plan's full interest waiver carried into the RAP transition for the auto-enrolled cohort. Documented errors include servicers posting the waiver one month and reversing it the next, applying the waiver to the wrong loan within a consolidation, and billing interest at the stated rate without the waiver adjustment. The waiver appears on the monthly statement as a separate line item; if yours is missing or inconsistent, that is a reportable error.

Autopay enrollment failure. Autopay requests submitted through the servicer dashboard were failing to process at measurable rates through October 2026, with some borrowers receiving confirmation emails but never seeing the first ACH debit, and others seeing ACH debits with no enrollment confirmation. Both scenarios jeopardize the 0.25 percent interest reduction and the broader on-time payment record. The SAVE Transition Guide walks the parallel autopay verification steps that should have happened at servicer transfer.

The CFPB Submission Flow Step by Step

The complaint lives at consumerfinance.gov/complaint. The flow is a guided form that takes 15 to 25 minutes if you have your evidence ready. Prepare the evidence first, then open the form; the form does not save partial submissions reliably and timing out loses your narrative.

Step 1: Select the product. On the first screen, choose "Student loan" as the product and then "Federal student loan servicing" as the sub-product. Do not select "Private student loan" even if your loan is a Direct Consolidation Loan; the correct designation is federal.

Step 2: Select the issue. The CFPB sub-category list is long; the three most relevant for 2026 servicing errors are "Dealing with your lender or servicer" (RAP payment amount, interest waiver, autopay), "Problem with a credit reporting company's investigation into an existing problem" (if your dispute is credit-reporting related), and "Received a loan I did not apply for" (rare; used when consolidation booking is contested). For the four error categories named in the November 5 letter, "Dealing with your lender or servicer" is correct.

Step 3: Write the narrative. The narrative field accepts up to 2,000 characters and is the single most important part of the complaint. Follow the four-paragraph structure described in the sample language section below. Specific dates, dollar amounts, servicer representative names when available, and reference numbers from prior calls materially improve the complaint's weight.

Step 4: Attach evidence. The CFPB form accepts up to 10 attachments totaling 25 MB. Attach the four documents listed in the evidence section below. If you have more than four relevant documents, prioritize: dated servicer correspondence first, account statements second, screenshots third, prior call logs fourth.

Step 5: Identify the servicer. The form asks for the company you are complaining about. Select the exact servicer name as it appears on your most recent billing statement: Mohela, Nelnet, Aidvantage (which is Maximus), EdFinancial, or ECSI. A complaint filed against the wrong servicer gets reassigned but delays the 60-day clock.

Step 6: Submit and save the confirmation number. On submission, the CFPB issues a complaint number starting with a date prefix (YYMMDD) and a seven-digit sequence. Screenshot the confirmation page or print to PDF. The number is required for any follow-up, rebuttal, or escalation.

The Four Pieces of Evidence That Change Complaint Outcomes

A complaint with specific attached evidence resolves at a 60 percent relief rate; a complaint without attachments resolves at closer to 30 percent. The four documents that matter most:

Evidence 1: The disputed servicer statement or dashboard screenshot. Export your most recent billing statement as a PDF directly from the servicer's dashboard. For dashboard-only data (like a PSLF count that is not on a statement), use a full-page screenshot with the URL bar visible and the system clock visible. Date-stamp the capture by having the system clock in the screenshot.

Evidence 2: Your calculation of the correct value. For RAP payment disputes, attach a one-page calculation showing your AGI, filing status, dependents, family size, tier, and the resulting monthly payment. For PSLF count disputes, attach your month-by-month employment timeline with the qualifying-plan designation per month. The calculation does not have to be elaborate; a plain PDF with the math documented counts.

Evidence 3: Prior servicer correspondence. Every email you have sent or received from the servicer about this issue should be attached as a PDF. If the only prior contact was by phone, write up a dated memo of each call including the date, time, duration, representative first name, reference number, and summary of what was said. A memo you write contemporaneously is admissible; a memo you write three months after the fact is weaker but still better than nothing.

Evidence 4: The regulatory citation. This is optional but powerful. Cite the specific regulation or policy your servicer has violated. For RAP, 34 CFR 685.209 (current version) sets the payment calculation; for PSLF, 34 CFR 685.219. For credit reporting violations, the Fair Credit Reporting Act's 30-day investigation requirement (15 USC 1681i). You do not need to know regulatory law in depth; one sentence naming the citation signals that your complaint is informed.

Sample Complaint Narrative That Gets Results

Adapt the template below to your situation. The structure is four paragraphs: facts, error, prior attempts, requested remedy. Keep the full narrative under 2,000 characters; the CFPB field truncates longer entries.

Paragraph 1, the facts: "I hold federal Direct Loans transferred to [servicer] on [transfer date]. I enrolled in the Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) effective [RAP effective date]. My current AGI per my 2025 federal tax return is $[amount]. My family size is [number], including [number] dependents."

Paragraph 2, the error: "My [monthly bill dated X / PSLF count report dated Y / interest statement dated Z] shows $[servicer figure]. Based on the 2026 RAP calculation under 34 CFR 685.209 at my AGI tier of [X percent] with the $50 per-dependent deduction applied, the correct payment should be $[correct figure]. The servicer's figure overstates my obligation by $[delta]."

Paragraph 3, the prior attempts: "I called [servicer] on [date] and spoke with representative [first name], reference number [number]. I was told [summary of response]. On [date] I sent a secure message through the servicer dashboard; I received [no response / this response]. The error has not been corrected."

Paragraph 4, the requested remedy: "I request (a) correction of the payment amount to $[correct figure] effective [date], (b) refund of overbilled amounts totaling $[amount] for [period], (c) written confirmation of the correction to my account, and (d) a notation on my account record that this CFPB complaint was filed and resolved."

The template works because it gives the servicer's compliance team everything they need to resolve the complaint without asking you for more information. Complaints that leave key figures as "I think it's wrong" without stating what the correct figure is close at substantially lower relief rates.

The 60-Day Response Timeline and What Happens Inside It

After you submit at consumerfinance.gov, the CFPB forwards the complaint to the servicer within 2 to 3 business days. The servicer has 15 calendar days to acknowledge receipt (usually a form letter saying "we received your complaint and are investigating") and up to 60 calendar days to provide a final written response.

During the 60-day window, the servicer's compliance or escalations team pulls your account, reviews the disputed transaction or figure, and prepares a written response that will become part of the CFPB record. This team is distinct from the general customer service line you may have called previously; CFPB complaints are routed to a dedicated response unit with regulatory reporting obligations. The practical consequence is that the response you receive through the CFPB is usually more thorough and more accurate than any answer you would get by calling customer service.

If the error is factual and documented, the servicer will typically correct it within the 60-day window and the response will note the correction. If the servicer believes its original action was correct, the response will explain the reasoning; you then have 60 days to submit a rebuttal through the CFPB portal. If the servicer does not respond at all within 60 days, the complaint is flagged "company did not respond" and becomes a line item in the CFPB's supervisory data, which is exactly the data congressional staff were citing in the November 5 letter to Secretary McMahon.

The Escalation Ladder If the Servicer Response Is Non-Responsive

Approximately 15 percent of CFPB student loan complaints close with "explanation" and no relief, and a smaller share close with an unresponsive answer that does not address the dispute. Three escalation channels in parallel maximize the odds of resolution:

Escalation 1: Submit a CFPB rebuttal. The CFPB portal lets you respond to the servicer's answer within 60 days of its posting. The rebuttal should name which specific statements in the servicer's response are factually inaccurate or non-responsive, what remedy you still require, and attach any additional evidence that has emerged since the initial complaint.

Escalation 2: File a state Student Loan Ombudsperson complaint. Nine states operate dedicated Student Loan Ombudsperson offices: New York, California, Illinois, Massachusetts, Washington, Virginia, Maryland, Connecticut, and Pennsylvania. These offices have state subpoena power and faster typical turnaround (30 to 45 days) than federal processes. For borrowers outside those nine states, the state attorney general's consumer protection division is the equivalent channel; for borrowers in any state, Letitia James's office at the NY AG has issued guidance that New York residents can file directly with EDCAP regardless of where the servicer is based.

Escalation 3: Congressional casework inquiry. Every member of Congress maintains constituent services staff whose entire job is filing Secretary of Education inquiries on behalf of constituents. Call or email your House representative and both senators; the casework intake typically takes 10 minutes. The congressional inquiry triggers a Department of Education response, usually within 21 days, that is separate from the CFPB and servicer channels. Congressional inquiries are particularly effective for PSLF count disputes and for errors that span multiple servicers.

The federal student loan ombudsman at feedback.studentaid.gov is a fourth channel that works in parallel. It is operated by the Department of Education itself and is useful for policy disputes (where a program rule is being applied incorrectly) more than for individual billing errors. File there as a supplement when the issue is a programmatic error affecting many borrowers.

The Bottom Line

The 33-lawmaker McMahon letter is political pressure built from exactly the kind of individual CFPB complaint record every borrower can add to. If your RAP payment is wrong, your PSLF count is wrong, your interest waiver reversed, or your autopay failed to enroll, the CFPB complaint flow at consumerfinance.gov forces a written servicer response within 60 days, produces a federal paper trail your servicer cannot ignore, and contributes a data point to the next round of congressional oversight. The complaint is free, takes 20 minutes to file, requires no attorney, and resolves at a 60 percent relief rate when properly documented.
