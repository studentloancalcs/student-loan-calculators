December 11 2026 Shutdown Deadline: The 24-Day Student Loan Action Checklist Before the Continuing Resolution Expires

The federal continuing resolution funding the government expires at 11:59 pm Eastern on December 11, 2026. Whether Congress reaches an agreement to extend funding or a lapse begins at midnight that night, student loan borrowers with time-sensitive filings have a 24-calendar-day window from November 17 to land those documents in servicer and Federal Student Aid queues before any potential processing slowdown. This guide covers the five highest-priority filings for the next 24 days, what stays running during a shutdown and what stops based on the October 2025 shutdown precedent, and how to protect PSLF months, RAP enrollment, default resolution progress, IDR applications, and consolidation submissions through any funding lapse that extends past year-end.

The continuing resolution signed in late September 2026 funds the federal government through December 11, 2026. Appropriations negotiations in the 24 days between now and the deadline will determine whether a longer funding bill or a short extension passes, or whether the government enters a funding lapse at 12:00 am on December 12. For most of the federal workforce, a shutdown is a payroll and operational issue. For student loan borrowers, it is a processing-queue issue: the Department of Education's Federal Student Aid staff pause their work during a lapse, and any filing that requires staff review (PSLF employer certifications, Total and Permanent Disability discharge applications, Ombudsman escalations, consolidation processing, borrower defense) sits in the queue until funding returns.

The October 2025 shutdown lasted 43 days and produced a visible backlog in PSLF employer certification processing, IDR recertification posting, and consolidation completion that took through early 2026 to clear. The playbook borrowers used then is the playbook that works again now: submit the time-sensitive filings before the deadline so they land in the queue, which locks the submission date and preserves credit for the underlying action (employment months, income update, consolidation intent) regardless of when Federal Student Aid staff open the file. Everything that does not require staff review (autopay, online payments, StudentAid.gov account access, interest accrual) continues uninterrupted. The 24-day window is about getting the right documents filed, not about trying to complete processing before the shutdown starts.

What Stays Running and What Stops

A government shutdown does not stop the student loan system; it slows the parts that require Department of Education employees to review and approve documents. Understanding which parts run and which parts stop focuses the 24-day window on the filings that actually matter.

What continues during a shutdown. Federal student loan servicers (MOHELA, Nelnet, Aidvantage, EdFinancial, CRI) remain open because they operate on existing contracts funded from borrower payment collections rather than annual appropriations. Autopay drafts continue. Mailed and online payments are accepted and applied to accounts. StudentAid.gov is accessible for account review, document download, and payment history pull. Interest accrual continues on all loans that are accruing interest. Credit bureau reporting on currently delinquent accounts continues on its scheduled cadence. The Repayment Assistance Plan's 100 percent monthly interest waiver and 50 percent principal match continue to post monthly for enrolled borrowers whose payments are current. Private student loan lenders are entirely unaffected because they operate outside the federal system.

What pauses during a shutdown. Federal Student Aid staff review of PSLF employer certification forms stops. Processing of consolidation applications slows significantly, with queue advancement limited to automated steps. Total and Permanent Disability discharge application review pauses. Closed-school discharge adjudication pauses. Borrower defense application processing pauses. Federal Student Aid Ombudsman Group case intake and resolution pauses. Federal Student Aid Information Center phone lines reduce hours or close entirely. Congressional inquiry response (your Representative's or Senator's casework office contacting Federal Student Aid on your behalf) pauses. IDR application review slows, though straightforward RAP annual recertifications typically continue because they run through servicer-level automation rather than Federal Student Aid staff review.

The 24-day window matters for anything in the "pauses" list. Filing before December 11 does not skip the queue if a shutdown happens, but it does lock the submission date and preserve credit for the underlying action, which keeps the borrower's calculation of months, dollars, and eligibility intact when processing resumes. Filing #1: PSLF Employer Certification (Due by December 1)

The PSLF employer certification form, submitted through the online PSLF Help Tool at studentaid.gov, records qualifying employment months with a 501(c)(3) nonprofit or qualifying government employer. Borrowers who have not submitted an employer certification form since August 2026, or who switched employers at any point in 2026, should file now rather than wait until January as many borrowers habitually do. The reason is specific: an October 2025 shutdown precedent form takes 60 to 120 days to complete processing in the normal queue, and a form filed on December 10 of a shutdown year typically does not post until mid-February. A form filed on November 30 or December 1 lands in the queue before the deadline and is the first batch processed when staff returns.

The practical PSLF deadline this year is December 1, not December 11, because the form requires the employer's HR representative to sign. HR offices typically slow their processing between December 10 and January 5 for the holiday period, and the employer-side signature is the step that takes longest in the PSLF workflow. Submit the form to your HR office the week of November 17 to 24, confirm the signature by December 1, and submit the completed form to Federal Student Aid that same week. The final submission step is instantaneous online; the employer signature step is the bottleneck. Verify your months estimate against the PSLF Tracker before and after submission.

Filing #2: RAP Annual Income Update for December and January Anniversaries

Borrowers whose RAP enrollment anniversary falls in December 2026 or January 2027 are facing their first annual income update since the plan's July 2026 launch. Many of these borrowers have not seen the RAP recertification notice yet because servicer notification timing has run 30 to 45 days behind the plan's regulatory schedule all year. The recertification itself is straightforward if the borrower has given IRS auto-recertification consent (which pulls income data from the 2025 tax return automatically); it is more time-sensitive if the borrower opted out of auto-recertification and must submit income documentation manually.

Borrowers with December or January anniversaries should log in to StudentAid.gov the week of November 17, check for any outstanding recertification notice, and complete the income update before December 11 even if the deadline the notice shows is later. The buffer protects against servicer-side processing slowdowns during a shutdown that would otherwise cause the payment amount to reset to a higher figure (not the 10-year standard, which is the SAVE-era reversion risk, but to a higher RAP amount based on prior-year income that no longer reflects current circumstances). The RAP Calculator can project both the current-income and prior-income scenarios to show the dollar difference.

Borrowers whose RAP anniversary falls in February, March, or later months are not in the 24-day urgency window for this specific filing, though the open-enrollment HSA and 401(k) moves that lower 2027 RAP payments are still worth completing before year-end for separate reasons. See the earlier guide on open-enrollment benefit elections and 2027 RAP payment for the full year-end AGI playbook.

Filing #3: Default Resolution Requests (Rehabilitation and Consolidation)

Defaulted federal student loan borrowers pursuing rehabilitation or consolidation out of default face the most acute shutdown risk among student loan borrower groups. Rehabilitation requires a signed written agreement with the Default Resolution Group that establishes the monthly payment amount under the 15 percent of discretionary income formula or the reasonable-and-affordable alternative. Consolidation out of default requires either three voluntary monthly payments followed by a consolidation application or immediate consolidation with an IDR plan election. Both pathways require Federal Student Aid staff review at specific checkpoints, and both pathways experience visible queue backlogs during shutdowns.

Borrowers in active default should submit the written rehabilitation request or the consolidation application before December 11. For rehabilitation, this means contacting the Default Resolution Group at 1-800-621-3115 to request the written offer letter and completing the reasonable-and-affordable documentation if the 15 percent formula produces a payment the borrower cannot afford. For consolidation out of default, this means filing the online consolidation application at studentaid.gov with IDR plan election (currently RAP for eligible loans or IBR for loans not eligible for RAP) completed in the same session.

The reason to file before December 11 is the August 2027 administrative wage garnishment schedule. The Department of Education's 2026 collections restart cadence puts the next major garnishment wave in August and September 2027, which means defaulted borrowers need their rehabilitation or consolidation complete by roughly July 2027 to avoid paycheck interception. A shutdown delay of 30 to 45 days in processing can push the completion date past that window. Filing in the next 24 days preserves the timeline. See the full rehabilitation timeline guide for the nine-month payment stream schedule.

Filing #4: IDR Recalculation for Income Changes

Borrowers whose income changed materially in 2026 (job loss, income drop from a career change, hours reduction, FMLA leave, divorce or separation) can request an off-cycle IDR payment recalculation at any time during the year without waiting for the annual recertification anniversary. The request is a one-page submission through the servicer's online portal or a signed paper form, supported by recent paystubs or an unemployment determination letter. Servicers typically process these in 30 to 45 days when staffing is normal; during a shutdown, the timeline stretches to 60 to 90 days even for servicer-side processing because servicers sometimes escalate complex cases to Federal Student Aid for guidance that is unavailable during a lapse.

Borrowers whose income is lower than the figure the servicer currently uses for the RAP or IBR payment calculation are overpaying each month until the recalculation posts. For a borrower whose income dropped from $75,000 to $45,000, the RAP payment difference is typically $150 to $250 per month. Filing the recalculation now rather than in January captures the correct payment amount for December, January, and February bills, while filing after December 11 risks the recalculation not posting until March or later. The RAP Calculator can quantify the overpayment for your specific balance and the before-and-after income figures.

The opposite situation also matters: borrowers whose income rose in 2026 are typically undercharged until recertification catches up at the annual anniversary, and have no urgency to file an off-cycle recalculation. For those borrowers, the 24-day window is not relevant to their IDR account; it is relevant to the other four filings.

Filing #5: Consolidation Submissions for Strategic Loan Grouping

Direct Loan Consolidation combines multiple federal loans into a single new Direct Consolidation Loan with a weighted-average interest rate and a new repayment plan election. Borrowers consolidate for specific reasons: moving non-Direct loans (FFEL, Perkins) into Direct status for PSLF eligibility, combining multiple serviced accounts under a single servicer, consolidating out of default, resetting consolidation-eligible loans for a RAP-eligible status, or simplifying parent PLUS loan management after the July 2026 regulatory changes.

Consolidation applications take 60 to 90 days to complete in normal processing and 90 to 150 days during and after a shutdown. The queue step that matters is the servicer handoff: the application is processed at a central Federal Student Aid consolidation unit, which then transfers the new consolidation loan to the borrower's chosen servicer for final booking. The handoff requires Federal Student Aid staff to execute the transfer, and the handoff pauses during a shutdown. An application filed on November 20 under normal processing might complete in late January; the same application filed on December 15 during a shutdown might not complete until April.

Borrowers considering consolidation for any of the five reasons above should decide before December 11 and file in the next 24 days if the answer is yes. Borrowers who are undecided should model both the consolidation and no-consolidation scenarios with the Plan Comparison tool, because once the decision is made the application itself is a 45-minute online form, not the bottleneck. One category of borrower should wait: anyone with a July 2026 PSLF-eligible consolidation that is still being processed should not file a new consolidation on top of the pending one, as this creates servicer-level confusion that takes months to resolve. Confirm the status of any pending consolidation at StudentAid.gov before filing a new application.

What Not to Do in the 24-Day Window

Four actions that look urgent but are not time-sensitive to the December 11 deadline. First, do not switch repayment plans mid-cycle unless the switch is required for one of the five filings above. A voluntary plan switch creates a payment-amount reset and servicer-side processing that adds complexity during a shutdown without a corresponding benefit. Second, do not file an Ombudsman complaint about a long-running issue unless you are also filing it with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau in parallel. A shutdown-timed Ombudsman complaint sits unopened for the duration of any lapse, while a CFPB complaint continues to be processed because CFPB funding is separate from Department of Education appropriations.

Third, do not refinance federal loans to private in response to shutdown anxiety. The November 2026 cashback promotions discussed in the private refinance 8-question test are still a one-way door that closes federal benefits permanently, and a short-term processing delay is a weak reason to accept the long-term optionality loss. Fourth, do not over-pay in a lump sum to "get ahead" of a shutdown. The pay-ahead status trap for RAP-enrolled borrowers applies regardless of whether a shutdown is pending; a lump-sum pay-ahead can forfeit the 50 percent principal match for months even in normal processing.

The November 2025 Precedent

The October 2025 shutdown offers the closest precedent for how a December 2026 lapse would affect student loan processing. During the 43-day lapse, Federal Student Aid confirmed in public statements that approximately 95 percent of the student loan system continued to operate: payment processing, autopay, online account access, servicer billing, interest accrual, and borrower correspondence received no interruption. The 5 percent that paused was staff-reviewed work: PSLF processing, TPD discharge, consolidation handoffs, Ombudsman intake, and FSA Information Center phone staffing. Following the shutdown's end, the processing backlog took approximately 60 to 90 days to clear, with PSLF count displays updating in waves through February and March 2026.

The practical takeaway from the 2025 precedent is that borrowers who filed their time-sensitive documents in the week before the shutdown had their underlying credit preserved (months, income updates, consolidation intent) even when processing took months to catch up. Borrowers who waited until after the shutdown started faced a longer total wait and a more complicated processing path because their file joined the backlog rather than sitting at the top of the queue when staff returned. The same pattern holds for 2026. The 24-day window from November 17 to December 11 is the window to file, not the window to complete.

If the Shutdown Does Not Happen

Appropriations negotiations in early December 2026 may produce a longer funding bill, a short extension of the continuing resolution past December 11, or a lapse. The five filings in this checklist are worth completing regardless of which outcome lands. PSLF certification months, RAP recertification timeliness, default resolution progress, IDR recalculation accuracy, and consolidation completion are all beneficial independent of any shutdown. The 24-day window simply converts "these are things you should do before year-end" into "these are things you should do before December 11 so they land in the queue before any potential processing slowdown." If no shutdown happens, the filings processed on their normal 30-to-90-day timeline. If a shutdown happens, the filings processed on a 60-to-150-day timeline, but the submission date locks the underlying credit either way.

For borrowers considering whether to complete the filings now versus wait until January after the shutdown question resolves: the January wait captures information (shutdown happened or did not) that the pre-December 11 filing does not have, but it forfeits the shutdown-resilient queue position for the time-sensitive filings that have shutdown risk. The optimal approach for most borrowers is to complete the five filings in the next 24 days and to defer the two or three optional January tasks (new year's budget review, annual credit report pull, student loan interest deduction tax prep) until the shutdown question has resolved and Federal Student Aid processing is back to normal.

The Bottom Line

The continuing resolution funding the federal government expires at 11:59 pm Eastern on December 11, 2026. Student loan borrowers have a 24-day window from November 17 to file five time-sensitive documents that must land in servicer and Federal Student Aid queues before a potential processing slowdown: a PSLF employer certification (practical deadline December 1 for HR signatures), RAP annual income update for December and January anniversaries, default resolution requests for borrowers pursuing rehabilitation or consolidation out of default, IDR off-cycle recalculation for material income changes in 2026, and consolidation submissions for strategic loan grouping. Autopay, servicer billing, online account access, interest accrual, and RAP interest waiver and principal match posting all continue during any shutdown. Staff-reviewed work (PSLF processing, TPD discharge, Ombudsman cases, consolidation handoffs) pauses. The October 2025 shutdown precedent showed that filings lodged before the lapse preserved borrower credit even when processing took 60 to 90 days to catch up after funding returned. The 24-day window is for filing, not for completion. For borrowers in the four filing categories above, the next three weeks are the correct time to act regardless of what Congress decides in early December.
