Tax-Loss Harvesting Before December 31 2026: How $3,000 of Capital Losses Lowers Your 2027 RAP Payment

Capital losses realized in a taxable brokerage account by December 31, 2026 reduce 2026 adjusted gross income, which is the figure the 2027 Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) payment is calculated on at the borrower's annual recertification. The federal $3,000 net capital loss deduction (the 26 USC 1211(b) annual cap for an individual filer) translates to roughly $300 in 2027 RAP payment savings at the 10 percent marginal rate, plus a multi-year carryforward that continues to reduce AGI until the loss is fully absorbed. The strategy has three moving parts: trade-date timing, the wash-sale 30-day window, and an interaction with married filing separately that most borrower-focused tax guides miss. This playbook walks all three for the six-week window between November 21 and December 31, 2026.

Tax-loss harvesting is a familiar year-end move for brokerage account holders: sell a position at a loss before December 31, offset realized capital gains elsewhere, and if net losses exceed net gains, deduct up to $3,000 of the excess against ordinary income on Schedule D. For federal student loan borrowers enrolled in the Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) or any income-driven plan, the strategy carries a second benefit that is not well covered in general tax-planning articles: the $3,000 deduction reduces AGI, and RAP payments scale with AGI above a protected income threshold. A full $3,000 deduction at the 10 percent RAP marginal rate is worth approximately $300 in reduced RAP payments across the 2027 plan year, which is 12 monthly payments of about $25 each. Net losses above $3,000 carry forward to 2027 and beyond, where they continue to reduce AGI at the next anniversary recertification and compound the benefit over multiple years. The six-week window between November 21 and December 31, 2026 is the practical tax-loss harvesting season. Brokerages process the largest volume of harvesting trades between December 15 and December 29, which creates settlement-cycle bottlenecks in the final days. Combined with brokerage holiday closures (most major U.S. brokerages close client service on December 24 afternoon, December 25 all day, and December 26 reduced staffing), the hard practical deadline for an executed trade with confirmed tax lot identification is Monday December 29, 2026 for anything other than a straight sell-at-market order. The IRS deadline of December 31 at the trade date is technically correct, but a trade executed December 31 at 12:59 pm Eastern that requires tax-lot identification (specific lot selection rather than default FIFO) may miss the brokerage's tax-lot confirmation cutoff and revert to the default accounting method, which in many brokerages is average-cost or FIFO and may surface a different gain or loss than intended.

The AGI Mechanism: How a Capital Loss Flows to the RAP Payment Calculation

The 2027 RAP payment calculation starts with the 2026 federal AGI on line 11 of Form 1040. Under the 2026 Reimagining and Improving Student Education (RISE) final regulations, RAP calculates the discretionary income base as AGI minus a protected income threshold set at 150 percent of the federal poverty guideline for family size, then applies a graduated marginal rate (currently 10 percent for most middle-income borrowers above the protected threshold, scaling to higher rates at higher income levels). A reduction in AGI translates directly to a reduction in discretionary income and therefore a reduction in the monthly payment. For a single filer with 2026 AGI of $60,000, a family size of one, and a federal poverty guideline of $15,650, the protected income threshold is $23,475 (150 percent of $15,650). Discretionary income is $36,525. The annual RAP payment at the 10 percent marginal rate is $3,652.50, or approximately $304 per month. A $3,000 capital loss deduction reduces AGI to $57,000 and discretionary income to $33,525. The annual payment drops to $3,352.50, or approximately $279 per month. The monthly savings is $25; the annual savings is $300.

For borrowers with larger loss positions (sometimes $10,000 or more in realized losses available), the full loss is not deductible against ordinary income in a single year because of the $3,000 annual cap. The remainder carries forward indefinitely as a continuing deduction on each subsequent year's Schedule D. A $15,000 realized loss in 2026 produces a $3,000 deduction in 2026 (lowering the 2027 RAP payment by $300), a $3,000 deduction in 2027 (lowering the 2028 RAP payment by $300), and so on for five years total. The compound benefit across five years is $1,500, which is meaningful relative to the typical year-end tax-planning effort. The Wash-Sale 30-Day Window: The Most Common Failure Point

The wash-sale rule under Internal Revenue Code Section 1091 is the single most common reason tax-loss harvesting produces less AGI reduction than the borrower planned. The rule disallows a capital loss if a substantially identical security is purchased within 30 days before or after the loss-generating sale, counted both directions. A loss harvested on December 15, 2026 is disallowed if the same security was purchased between November 15 and November 30, 2026 (the 30-day before window) or if the same security is purchased between December 16, 2026 and January 14, 2027 (the 30-day after window). The disallowed loss is not permanently lost; it is added to the cost basis of the replacement purchase, which defers the deduction rather than eliminating it. But for the 2026 tax year AGI calculation and therefore the 2027 RAP payment, a wash-sale-disallowed loss produces no benefit.

Three practical traps catch most borrowers. First, automatic dividend reinvestment (DRIP) during the 30-day window counts as a purchase and can trigger a partial wash sale on a small fraction of the lot. Turn off DRIP on the harvested security before the sale and leave it off through January 14, 2027. Second, the IRS defines substantially identical broadly for mutual funds and ETFs tracking the same index. Selling VTI and buying ITOT within 30 days typically triggers the wash-sale rule because both are total-U.S.-market index funds. The standard workaround is to swap to a different asset class (e.g., selling VTI and buying a bond fund or a sector ETF during the 30-day window) or to a different index entirely (e.g., selling VTI and buying VXF, an extended-market fund with meaningfully different composition). Third, the wash-sale rule applies across accounts owned by the same taxpayer and across the taxpayer's spouse's accounts if the couple files jointly. A loss harvested in a taxable brokerage account is disallowed if the same security is purchased in an IRA, a 401(k) through a brokerage window, or the spouse's account within the 30-day window.

Tax-Lot Identification: Picking Which Shares to Sell

Most brokerage accounts hold multiple purchase lots of the same security at different cost bases. A position in VTI purchased monthly over 2024, 2025, and 2026 contains 36 separate tax lots at different prices. Selling 100 shares of VTI without specifying which lots are sold defaults to the brokerage's default accounting method, which for most major brokerages is average cost (for mutual funds) or FIFO (for ETFs and individual stocks). For tax-loss harvesting, the borrower wants to sell the highest-cost-basis lots to maximize the realized loss, which requires specific lot identification at trade entry. Most major brokerages (Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard, E-Trade, Robinhood) allow specific lot selection through the trade entry screen or through a separate tax-lot management interface.

The practical constraint is that specific-lot selection must be confirmed before the trade settles, which is T+1 for U.S. equities and ETFs. A trade executed December 29, 2026 settles December 30 and the specific-lot designation must be confirmed before December 30 close of business. A trade executed December 31 at market close has specific-lot designation confirmed by January 2, 2027, which is still within the 2026 tax year for Form 1099-B reporting purposes but creates operational risk if the brokerage's tax-lot system is backlogged in the final days of December. The safer path is to execute harvesting trades no later than Monday December 29, 2026 and confirm specific-lot selection the same day.

The Married Filing Separately Interaction

Borrowers running the married filing separately (MFS) strategy for RAP (to exclude the spouse's income from the RAP payment calculation) face a specific allocation question: which spouse's return captures the capital loss? The answer depends on account titling and state law. For an individual brokerage account titled solely in the borrower's name, the full loss flows to the borrower's MFS return, which captures the full AGI reduction benefit. For a joint brokerage account in a non-community-property state, the loss is typically split 50/50 between the spouses, meaning only $1,500 of the annual $3,000 cap flows to the borrower's MFS return ($1,500 is the MFS individual cap, not $3,000). For a community property state (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, Wisconsin), community property rules typically split the loss 50/50 regardless of titling unless the account was funded with traced separate property from before the marriage.

Borrowers running the MFS strategy should concentrate harvestable losses in an individual account titled solely in the borrower's name before December 31, 2026 to capture the full benefit. For couples with a joint account, the practical move is to transfer harvestable positions to an individual account in the borrower's name before the sale, then execute the loss trade from the individual account. The transfer itself is not a taxable event, but the tax lot and holding period carry over to the receiving account. Confirm the transfer and tax-lot carryover with the brokerage before executing the sale. The Short-Term vs. Long-Term Character Question

Capital losses are first netted within their holding-period class: short-term losses (securities held one year or less) offset short-term gains, long-term losses (securities held more than one year) offset long-term gains. Any remaining net loss of one character offsets net gain of the other character. The final net capital loss (whatever character it ends up as) is deducted against ordinary income up to the $3,000 annual cap, and the character of the deduction is irrelevant at the ordinary-income step.

For tax-loss harvesting with a RAP payment objective, the holding-period character matters less than the gross realized loss amount. However, two optimizations are available. First, if the borrower has unrealized short-term gains (positions held less than one year), harvesting a short-term loss offsets the short-term gain at ordinary income rates (up to 37 percent plus state) rather than at long-term capital gains rates (up to 20 percent plus state). Harvesting long-term losses to offset short-term gains is tax-efficient. Second, if the borrower expects to realize long-term gains in 2027 (for example, a planned house down payment or a portfolio rebalancing), banking a short-term loss carryforward preserves the loss to offset the 2027 long-term gain. The character of the carryforward is preserved: short-term carries as short-term, long-term carries as long-term.

State Conformity: What Does and Does Not Follow

State capital loss treatment varies and does not affect the federal RAP calculation. The RAP payment is calculated on federal AGI (Form 1040 line 11), which uses the federal $3,000 annual cap. States that conform to federal rules (most states) produce the same AGI figure on the state return. States that deviate (California does not conform to the federal $3,000 cap for all taxpayers and has its own carryforward rules; Pennsylvania does not allow capital loss carryforwards at all for individual income tax) produce a different state AGI, but the federal AGI used for RAP is unaffected.

For borrowers in high-tax states (California, New York, New Jersey, Hawaii, Oregon), the combined federal-plus-state value of the $3,000 deduction can exceed the RAP payment reduction, which makes the harvesting trade worthwhile even for borrowers not on an income-driven plan. For RAP borrowers in high-tax states, the combined benefit is layered: federal tax savings at the marginal income tax rate, state tax savings at the state marginal rate, and RAP payment reduction at the 10 percent (or higher) marginal rate. A borrower in California at the 24 percent federal bracket and the 9.3 percent California bracket realizes combined tax savings of 33.3 percent on $3,000, or $999, plus $300 in RAP payment reduction, for a total benefit of $1,299 on a $3,000 harvesting trade.

The Six-Week Execution Timeline (November 21 to December 31, 2026)

Week of November 21 to November 28, 2026: Inventory harvestable positions. Pull year-to-date realized gain and loss report from brokerage. List all positions with unrealized losses greater than $500 (the minimum threshold below which tax-loss harvesting typically does not justify the transaction cost and tracking overhead). Identify replacement securities for each harvestable position that are not substantially identical. Turn off DRIP on harvestable positions.

Week of November 30 to December 5, 2026: Confirm account titling for MFS strategy. If running MFS, confirm which positions sit in individual versus joint accounts. For couples in non-community-property states, consider transferring harvestable positions to an individual account titled in the student loan borrower's name. Transfers between accounts at the same brokerage typically process in 1 to 3 business days; transfers between brokerages via ACATS can take 5 to 10 business days.

Week of December 7 to December 12, 2026: Execute initial round of harvesting trades. Avoid the final two weeks of December if possible, when settlement bottlenecks and tax-lot system load peak. Select specific tax lots at trade entry. Confirm tax-lot selection on the brokerage tax-lot management screen within 24 hours of the trade.

Week of December 14 to December 19, 2026: Execute remaining harvesting trades. Confirm no DRIP activity or wash-sale-triggering purchases occurred since the first-round trades. Monitor for any inadvertent wash sales across all accounts (individual, joint, IRA, spouse's accounts).

Week of December 22 to December 29, 2026: Final cleanup. Hard deadline for executed trades is Monday December 29, 2026 with specific-lot selection confirmed the same day. Avoid December 30 and December 31 for any non-trivial harvesting trade because brokerage tax-lot systems are typically backlogged in the final two days.

Week of January 5 to January 16, 2027: Wash-sale window closes January 14, 2027 for trades executed December 15, 2026. After January 14, the position can be repurchased without wash-sale consequences if desired. For trades executed later in December, extend the window accordingly (December 20 trade: avoid repurchase through January 19; December 29 trade: avoid repurchase through January 28).

The Bottom Line

Capital losses realized in a taxable brokerage account by December 31, 2026 reduce 2026 adjusted gross income, which is the figure the 2027 Repayment Assistance Plan payment is calculated on at the borrower's annual recertification. A full $3,000 net capital loss deduction (the individual annual cap under 26 USC 1211(b); $1,500 for MFS) reduces the 2027 annual RAP payment by approximately $300 for a borrower at the 10 percent marginal rate, with the remainder carried forward to 2027 and beyond for continued AGI reduction in future plan years. The strategy has three principal failure modes: a wash-sale violation within the 30-day window before or after the sale, incorrect tax-lot selection that surfaces a different gain or loss than intended, and joint-account titling that splits the loss between spouses in an MFS household.
