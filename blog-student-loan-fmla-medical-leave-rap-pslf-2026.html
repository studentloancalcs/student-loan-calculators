Student Loan Payments During FMLA and Medical Leave in Late 2026: How Short-Term Disability Pay Affects Your RAP Amount, Whether PSLF Keeps Counting, and the One Mistake That Triggers Default

Open enrollment season is also planning season for the leaves people take in the following twelve months: maternity and parental leave, a knee replacement, a cancer workup, a parent moving into hospice, a disability that nobody saw coming. For federal student loan borrowers, protected leave is where a tidy repayment plan quietly comes apart. This article walks through the three kinds of medical leave, how short-term disability payouts and state paid family leave benefits show up (or do not) in Adjusted Gross Income, how RAP's early recertification rule drops the monthly payment to $10 within one billing cycle, whether PSLF keeps counting during protected leave, and the one documentation mistake that routinely converts a protected leave into a 270-day default.

Student loan servicers do not get a notification when a borrower goes on FMLA. Autopay does not reconfigure itself when a paycheck drops from full to partial. The Education Department has no automated hardship suspension triggered by a short-term disability claim. The entire protected-leave infrastructure is federal employment law (FMLA, USERRA, Pregnant Workers Fairness Act, Americans with Disabilities Act accommodations) and private insurance (STD, long-term disability, hospital indemnity), with state paid family and medical leave programs layered on top in roughly a dozen states. None of it talks to the student loan servicer. The result: a borrower who assumes their job protection also protects their loan payment often learns otherwise three months after the first missed payment, when the sixty-day delinquency letter arrives at a mailbox nobody has been checking.

The Three Kinds of Medical Leave and How Each Affects Your Loan

FMLA (unpaid, up to twelve weeks). The federal Family and Medical Leave Act protects a job for up to twelve weeks of unpaid leave per rolling twelve-month period for employees at employers with fifty or more workers who have worked at least 1,250 hours in the prior year. FMLA is job protection and health insurance protection; it is not income protection. During FMLA weeks the paycheck typically drops to zero unless the employee elects to use accrued paid time off, parental leave, or STD benefits to replace some portion of pay. For federal student loan borrowers, the key fact is that no student loan rule triggers off FMLA status; the monthly payment remains due as scheduled.

Short-term disability (partial pay, 60-90 percent of wages). Short-term disability insurance replaces a percentage of wages during a defined period of inability to work, typically six to twenty-six weeks. STD policies vary dramatically: group plans at the employer run through an insurance carrier like Unum, The Hartford, or Lincoln Financial, and voluntary plans let employees elect a benefit percentage (60, 66.67, 70, or 80 percent) with corresponding premium costs. Whether the STD payout appears on a W-2 depends entirely on who paid the premium and with what tax treatment; see the Section 125 trap below. For RAP purposes, taxable STD income raises AGI and (eventually) the monthly payment; non-taxable STD income does not.

State paid family and medical leave (PFML, partial wage replacement). As of 2026, roughly a dozen states and the District of Columbia operate paid family and medical leave programs funded by payroll tax: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New Jersey, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, Washington, and D.C., plus Puerto Rico. Benefit levels range from 60 to 90 percent of average weekly wages up to a cap. Most state PFML benefits are federally taxable (reported on W-2 or 1099-G depending on state administration) and enter AGI in the year received. The exceptions and nuances are state-specific; a Massachusetts PFML payment of $900 per week is reported as taxable wages and enters RAP's AGI calculation at the next recertification.

The Section 125 Trap: Pre-Tax Premiums Make Benefits Taxable

The biggest invisible decision borrowers make at open enrollment is whether to pay short-term disability premiums with pre-tax or post-tax dollars. Many employers offer the choice through a Section 125 cafeteria plan election, and the default is often pre-tax (because it reduces the current paycheck tax bill by a small amount). The trade-off: a pre-tax premium makes the eventual STD payout fully taxable; a post-tax premium makes the payout fully tax-free. For most workers the pre-tax election saves perhaps $30 to $80 per year in payroll tax. The cost of that election: if the borrower actually uses the STD policy during a twelve-week leave at $800 per week, the $9,600 payout is fully taxable and enters AGI; the post-tax alternative would have delivered the same $9,600 tax-free.

For RAP borrowers specifically, the AGI effect compounds the pay effect. A $9,600 taxable STD payout raises AGI by $9,600, which raises the following year's RAP payment calculation by $80 per month (1 percent of $9,600 under RAP's lower tier for a borrower with AGI between the protected income floor and $37,500) or $160 per month (under RAP's second tier). The pre-tax STD election is a classic case of saving $50 to lose $960 later. Borrowers heading into open enrollment during the November 2026 cycle who anticipate a possible leave in the following year should switch STD premium elections to post-tax; the switch is a one-check-box change in the enrollment portal and the tax savings over a single leave year easily cover the slightly higher premium cost.

RAP's Early Recertification Rule Is the Primary Tool

RAP, like IBR and the pre-2026 income-driven plans, lets a borrower whose current income has dropped from the income on the most recent approved application submit an updated application at any time rather than waiting for the annual recertification anniversary. The new monthly payment is recalculated using the updated income and takes effect for the next billing cycle, typically one to two months after the application is approved. The application is at studentaid.gov under the Income-Driven Repayment menu, and the required documentation is any one of: the two most recent pay stubs (which during unpaid FMLA show $0), an employer letter stating the leave period and pay status, or a signed self-certification of unemployment or reduced income if the borrower is otherwise self-attesting.

For most borrowers on unpaid FMLA, the updated application produces the $10 RAP minimum payment. The $10 is the floor; AGI at $0 or any amount below the protected income threshold (roughly 150 percent of the Federal Poverty Line for 2026: $23,475 single, $31,845 family of two, $40,215 family of three, $48,585 family of four) all result in the same $10 minimum. The 50 percent principal match credits $5 of the $10 to principal and the interest waiver zeroes out the remaining accrued interest for the month, so the $10 payment is a durable arrangement even for a leave of several months. For borrowers whose STD or PFML benefits are taxable and large, the payment may be above $10 but still substantially below the pre-leave amount; running the exact math is what the RAP Calculator was built for.

PSLF Credit During Protected Leave

For borrowers in Public Service Loan Forgiveness, the question during leave is not whether the employer counts (it does, as long as the employee is still on the roster at a qualifying 501(c)(3), government, or AmeriCorps-eligible employer) but whether the payment counts. PSLF requires 120 qualifying monthly payments, and a qualifying payment is one made on an income-driven plan while employed at a qualifying employer. The payment made during FMLA at $10 per month under RAP counts as a qualifying payment, so the 120-month clock keeps advancing. A missed payment (delinquency), a payment during forbearance, or a payment during deferment generally does not count, with narrow exceptions for cancer treatment deferment, AmeriCorps deferment, and the one-time pre-2024 account adjustment that is no longer available for new claims.

The employer's PSLF Employer Certification Form (ECF) will show the borrower as continuously employed during protected leave; the employer reports the leave in a separate box but the headline employment status is "continuously employed." For borrowers planning leave in late 2026 or 2027 who are inside the final year or two of their PSLF 120-month window, confirming the ECF treatment with HR before the leave starts is worth the ten-minute conversation. A Worked Example: Twelve-Week FMLA with Partial STD Pay

Consider a 34-year-old nurse at a 501(c)(3) hospital with $62,000 in federal student loans, currently on RAP with a $185 monthly payment, 78 PSLF payments completed. She is six weeks away from a scheduled C-section and plans twelve weeks of FMLA. Her employer offers short-term disability at 70 percent of weekly wages for the first six weeks of post-partum medical leave (the standard medical component of maternity leave in states without statutory PFML), after which the final six weeks are unpaid unless she uses accrued PTO. Her weekly wage is $1,615; her STD benefit will be $1,130 per week for six weeks ($6,780 total). She elected pre-tax STD premiums at open enrollment two years ago.

The naive plan: continue autopay at $185 per month, use PTO for the second six weeks, hope the paycheck arithmetic works. In practice, autopay fails or is manually paused during the first STD week when the paycheck shrinks below the autopay deduction amount; the servicer sends a 15-day delinquency notice to her mailing address, which she does not open. Two more missed payments, two more notices. At the 90-day mark the delinquency is reported to the credit bureaus and her FICO drops 85 points. At month six of unresolved delinquency she returns from leave to a flagged account, three missed PSLF payments, and a credit profile she will need to repair before her upcoming mortgage refinance.

The better plan: in week one of maternity leave, submit the early RAP recertification using the current pay stub (which shows the STD transition and reduced gross) as income evidence. The RAP payment drops to roughly $55 per month for the twelve-week leave period (STD income enters AGI projection at $6,780 taxable, plus any PTO wages, plus year-to-date regular wages pro-rated). She continues to make $55 payments on autopay from a backup checking account, PSLF counts all twelve months, the forgiveness clock keeps ticking, and she returns from leave with a clean payment history and no credit damage. After returning to work she either waits for the annual recertification anniversary (payment stays at $55 until then, which is a bonus from the leave) or submits another early recertification at the new (full) income if she wants to be strictly current. The total out-of-pocket during leave is $165 ($55 times three months) instead of $555 for the pre-leave payment; the saved $390 covers the AGI effect from the taxable STD election with room to spare.

The One-Phone-Call Checklist for Week One of Leave

The thirty-minute task that prevents nearly all protected-leave default scenarios:

1. Call your student loan servicer (MOHELA, Nelnet, Aidvantage, EdFinancial, ECSI, or whichever services your Direct Loans in late 2026). Confirm current balance, current payment amount, autopay source account, and next due date. Ask them to flag your account as "leave of absence in progress" so any delinquency notices route to a specific case rather than form letters.

2. Submit the early RAP recertification at studentaid.gov with the current pay stub showing the income change. Confirmation emails arrive within two business days; the new payment amount posts within one to two billing cycles.

3. Confirm autopay works from the account you expect to have funds in during leave. If direct deposit of normal wages pauses, autopay from the operating checking account may bounce; move autopay to a joint account, a backup account, or an account linked to a partner's paycheck.

4. Request a mailing address update if you are moving in with family during leave or your mailing address is otherwise unreliable. Student loan servicers mail paper notices for delinquency, servicer transfer, and interest rate changes; a missed notice is often the start of the default spiral.

5. If PSLF-tracking, email your HR for a statement that you remain in qualifying employment during the leave period. File it with your other PSLF records. At the next annual ECF submission, HR will fill in the leave period correctly without additional prompting.

6. If the leave is unexpected (sudden illness, injury, hospitalization of a dependent) and you cannot do the paperwork yourself, authorize a family member or trusted contact in writing to speak with the servicer. Student loan servicers accept written authorization at any time; the form is on each servicer's website under "Account Access."

What Not to Do

Do not treat FMLA as a loan pause. FMLA protects the job; nothing in federal statute pauses a student loan during protected leave. Autopay does not reconfigure itself. The servicer does not know you are on leave.

Do not default to forbearance. Forbearance pauses PSLF and RAP forgiveness clocks and typically capitalizes accrued interest at the end. The early RAP recertification to $10 is almost always better. The narrow forbearance exceptions are listed in the FAQ above.

Do not assume state PFML is tax-free. Most state PFML benefits are federally taxable and enter AGI. Confirm with your state's Department of Labor or Family Leave administrator; the 1099-G or W-2 treatment is what RAP will use next year.

Do not elect pre-tax STD premiums at open enrollment if you reasonably expect to use the benefit. The pre-tax saving is small; the AGI effect from a taxable payout is large. Our open enrollment benefit elections guide walks through the pre-tax versus post-tax election for STD, HSA, 401(k), dependent care FSA, and commuter benefits.

Do not skip the early recertification because you expect leave to be short. Even a four-week leave at $0 pay stub can qualify, and the lower RAP payment is retained through the end of the recertification cycle, which can be another nine or ten months. The paperwork is roughly fifteen minutes online.

The Bottom Line

Protected leave is a planning event, not a payment pause. The federal student loan servicer has no visibility into your FMLA calendar, your short-term disability claim, your state PFML application, or your post-partum recovery. The borrower who assumes "protected job" means "protected loan" is the borrower who returns from leave to a 180-day delinquency, three missed PSLF payments, and a credit profile that needs repair. The borrower who makes one thirty-minute phone call in week one of leave, submits the RAP early recertification to the $10 minimum (or the actual STD-adjusted amount), confirms autopay from a backup account, and emails HR for a leave-period employment letter returns to a clean payment history, uninterrupted PSLF credit, and a lower RAP payment that persists through the next recertification anniversary.

The arithmetic of avoiding default is unusually clear: thirty minutes of paperwork in week one versus a seven-year credit scar, missed PSLF months that cannot be bought back under the 2026 rulebook, and the loss of the RAP interest waiver and principal match for every forbearance month. The $10 RAP payment is not a hardship; it is a design feature. Using it during leave is the plan.
