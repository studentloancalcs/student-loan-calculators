Injured Spouse Form 8379 for Defaulted Student Loan Borrowers: The November 2026 Playbook to Protect Your Spouse's 2027 Tax Refund From Treasury Offset

Treasury Offset of federal tax refunds restarted for defaulted student loans in 2026, and January 2027 is the first full tax-filing season under restored collections. For married-filing-joint borrowers whose spouse is not on the defaulted debt, Form 8379 Injured Spouse Allocation is the one mechanism that keeps the non-defaulted spouse's share of the joint refund from being seized. Here is the full November prep playbook: who qualifies, when to file, the processing-time math, the community-property-state carve-out, a worked example, and the five mistakes that lose the allocation.

Treasury Offset for defaulted federal student loans was paused during the pandemic and did not resume until the Education Department referred the first batch of defaulted accounts to the Treasury Offset Program in summer 2026. The 65-day pre-offset notice window closed in October for the first wave, and by late November 2026 roughly 1.5 million borrowers in default have received, or will shortly receive, notice that their 2026 federal income tax refund is subject to offset. The IRS begins accepting 2026 returns in the last week of January 2027; refunds that would otherwise land as deposits in February are the first that run through the Treasury Offset Program filter.

For a borrower in default who files single, the arithmetic is simple: the refund is seized up to the balance of the defaulted debt. For a borrower in default who files married-filing-joint, the arithmetic cuts both ways. The joint refund is a single pool that Treasury can offset against one spouse's defaulted loans, which means a non-defaulted spouse who contributed most of the withholding or qualifies for refundable credits of their own loses their share by default. Form 8379, Injured Spouse Allocation, is the IRS mechanism that reverses this result. Filed correctly, it carves the non-defaulted spouse's portion out of the joint refund before offset is applied, and that portion is released as a separate payment.

Who Qualifies as an Injured Spouse

The IRS defines an injured spouse as a joint filer who (1) is not personally liable for the past-due debt being collected, (2) had earned income or made tax payments during the year, and (3) would have received a refund if the debt had not been applied against the joint return. All three conditions must be met. A spouse who co-signed the defaulted loan or whose name appears on the promissory note is not an injured spouse; the loan is their obligation too. A spouse who had zero earned income, zero withholding, and no refundable credits of their own has no refund share to protect and does not qualify. A spouse whose own return would have produced a balance owed rather than a refund is not injured even if their partner's offset exceeds the joint refund.

The injured spouse must sign the form personally. The obligated spouse (the one with the defaulted loan) does not sign Form 8379 at all. If you are the defaulted-loan borrower, your spouse files 8379 against the joint return and signs it alone. Nothing about the filing requires the obligated spouse's consent; it is the injured spouse's statutory right.

When to File: With the Return or After the Offset

Form 8379 can be filed in three ways, and the choice changes both the processing time and the amount of money that gets held up. The three paths:

Path 1: Attached to the Original Return (Recommended)

File Form 8379 as an attachment to the joint 1040 when the return is first submitted. The IRS processes the allocation before any offset referral is forwarded to Treasury. Processing time is approximately 11 weeks for an e-filed return and 14 weeks for a paper return. The injured spouse's portion of the refund is issued separately once allocation is complete. This is the fastest and cleanest path, and it is what the IRS instructions explicitly recommend. If you file this way, the entire joint refund is held during processing; nothing is offset in the interim.

Path 2: Mailed Separately After the Return Is Already Filed

If the joint return went in without Form 8379 and the full refund was offset or is about to be offset, file Form 8379 by itself on paper to the IRS service center that processed the original return. Processing time is approximately 8 weeks from receipt. This path is slower in calendar terms (the return itself has already gone through the system and been offset) but faster in incremental IRS processing time. Use it if you only learned about the offset after filing.

Path 3: With an Amended Return

If other return errors require an amended 1040-X anyway, Form 8379 can be attached to it. This is uncommon and adds complexity. Most borrowers should use Path 1 or Path 2.

The practical rule for the 2027 tax season: if you know you or your spouse has a federal student loan in default, file Form 8379 attached to the original return. Do not wait for the offset notice to prove the risk is real; the pre-offset notice is the IRS confirming what you already know, and waiting adds 8 weeks and a seized refund you then have to claw back.

The Allocation Math: How Form 8379 Part III Works

Form 8379 Part III is a three-column worksheet that splits the joint 1040 into an injured-spouse column, an obligated-spouse column, and a joint-allocation column. For a typical dual-income W-2 couple, the five-step allocation is:

Step 1: Allocate income. Each spouse's W-2 wages, Schedule C self-employment income, interest, dividends, and capital gains go in that spouse's column based on whose Social Security number appears on the source form (W-2, 1099, Schedule C). Joint investment accounts are split by whose SSN appears on the 1099 or 50/50 by default if the account is jointly titled.

Step 2: Allocate adjustments to income. Student loan interest deduction (which you can model with the RAP Calculator to understand how your 2026 payments factor in), traditional IRA deduction, HSA deduction, and SEP contributions go in the column of the spouse whose name is on the underlying account. If both spouses contributed to separate HSAs, each spouse's deduction goes in their own column.

Step 3: Allocate deductions. The standard deduction is split 50/50 unless records support a different allocation. Itemized deductions follow the paper trail: mortgage interest on a jointly titled home splits 50/50 (or by actual payment records); property taxes split the same way; charitable giving goes to whoever wrote the check from a non-joint account.

Step 4: Allocate dependents and exemptions. Children are typically allocated by agreement; the default is to assign each dependent to whichever parent produces the larger refund protection. For a defaulted-loan couple, assigning dependents to the injured spouse maximizes the Child Tax Credit allocated to their column.

Step 5: Allocate credits and payments. Child Tax Credit, Earned Income Tax Credit, education credits, and federal withholding each go in the column of the spouse whose income or dependents produced them. The IRS then computes each column as a hypothetical single-filer return, and the injured spouse's refund equals their payments minus their standalone tax liability. That refund is released as a separate payment; the obligated spouse's share is forwarded to Treasury Offset.

The Community-Property-State Carve-Out

Nine states apply community property law to marital income: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin. In these states, income earned by either spouse during the marriage is community property under state law, which the IRS respects for Form 8379 allocation. The practical effect is that W-2 wages, withholding, and most credits are split 50/50 by law, not by whose SSN produced them. The injured spouse in a community property state typically recovers about half the joint refund even when they earned most of the income, because the state treats the earnings as jointly owned.

There is one wrinkle: the Treasury Offset Program must apply the state's community property rules, which sometimes carve out separate property (gifts, inheritances, income from pre-marriage separate property). An injured spouse in a community property state who received a significant inheritance that generated the refund-producing income can argue separate-property treatment for that specific stream. The IRS applies state law case-by-case; a letter from a state tax practitioner attached to the 8379 helps.

Worked Example: $6,400 Joint Refund, One Defaulted Loan

Taylor and Jordan file jointly for 2026. Taylor earned $82,000 of W-2 wages with $9,800 federal withholding; Jordan earned $38,000 of W-2 wages with $3,100 federal withholding. They have two children. Jordan has $48,000 of federal student loans in default, with a 2024 account referral to the Treasury Offset Program and an October 2026 pre-offset notice. The joint return shows a $6,400 refund.

Without Form 8379, Treasury would offset the entire $6,400 against Jordan's defaulted balance in February 2027. With Form 8379 attached to the e-filed joint return, the IRS runs the allocation worksheet. Taylor's column shows $82,000 income, $9,800 withholding, and both Child Tax Credits ($4,400 for two children at $2,200 each in 2026). Jordan's column shows $38,000 income, $3,100 withholding, and no CTC. Taylor's standalone tax liability on $82,000 is approximately $8,900 (2026 single-filer brackets with standard deduction). Taylor's refund share: $9,800 withholding + $4,400 CTC − $8,900 tax = $5,300. That $5,300 is released to Taylor as the injured spouse. Jordan's $1,100 remainder flows to Treasury Offset. The net result: Taylor keeps 83% of what the joint refund would have paid in cash, Jordan's defaulted debt gets reduced by $1,100 instead of $6,400, and the household loses $5,300 less than if 8379 had not been filed.

The parallel decision Taylor and Jordan should model is whether to file married-filing-separately (MFS) instead. Separate filing shields Taylor's refund completely but typically increases combined tax liability by $800 to $2,500 for a couple at this income level, and it disqualifies Taylor from the student loan interest deduction and reduces the Child Tax Credit refundable portion. For Jordan on RAP or IBR, MFS could lower the student loan payment, but Jordan is in default and not on an income-driven plan. In this case, married-filing-joint with Form 8379 attached is almost always the better path: it saves Taylor's refund share and still produces the lower joint tax liability.

The Five Mistakes That Lose the Allocation

1. Both spouses sign the form. Only the injured spouse signs. The obligated spouse's signature is not required and can confuse processing. If the form arrives with both signatures and no clear injured-spouse column, the IRS may bounce it back.

2. Allocating all withholding to the injured spouse. Withholding follows the W-2. Shifting the obligated spouse's withholding into the injured spouse's column is an audit flag and triggers a rejection or allocation adjustment. Each W-2's withholding goes in the column of the SSN on the W-2.

3. Missing Line 5a or 5b (community property box). Filers in Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin must check Line 5a or 5b indicating the state. Filers in the other 41 states check neither. A missed box in a community property state causes the IRS to apply the default non-community rules, which usually gives the injured spouse MORE refund than they are legally entitled to; the IRS corrects it and the processing time stretches.

4. Filing before the return is accepted. If 8379 is e-filed as a stand-alone before the joint 1040, it is rejected. Attach it as part of the original e-file bundle or paper-file it as part of the return.

5. Forgetting to re-file 8379 for the next tax year. Form 8379 is a per-return filing. If Jordan's loan remains in default through 2027, Taylor must file another Form 8379 with the 2027 return filed in 2028. There is no multi-year protection; each year is independent.

The November Prep Checklist

Three November tasks set up a clean January 2027 filing. First, confirm the loan is actually in default at studentaid.gov under the loan status page; a RAP, IBR, or current standard-plan loan is not referred to Treasury Offset, and the question of whether to run the plan comparison is more valuable than filing 8379 unnecessarily. Second, gather both spouses' 2026 pay-stub year-to-date withholding totals, which previews what each W-2 will show in late January. Third, decide between Path 1 (file 8379 with the return) and MFS by running both scenarios in a tax-prep tool: a $500 difference in combined tax between MFJ-plus-8379 and MFS, with 8379 recovering $5,000, makes the choice obvious.

If the defaulted loans are the result of a dispute (identity theft, servicer error, loans you believe were already paid off), the parallel November task is to get those loans out of default status before January through a rehabilitation or consolidation agreement. A loan that leaves default status by the time the IRS begins the offset run in February is not referred to Treasury that cycle, and Form 8379 is not needed.

The Bottom Line

Treasury Offset is real, back in effect for the January 2027 filing season, and will take the full joint refund of a defaulted-loan couple who files without protection. Form 8379 Injured Spouse Allocation is the one tool that protects the non-defaulted spouse's share, and it is a routine attachment the IRS processes in 11 weeks when e-filed with the return. The two decisions that matter are made in November, not in January: confirm loan status at studentaid.gov, and compare MFJ-plus-8379 against MFS so that whichever path the couple chooses in January is the one that actually leaves the household with more money.
