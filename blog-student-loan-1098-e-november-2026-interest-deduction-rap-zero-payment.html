Student Loan 1098-E November 2026 Prep: How to Claim the $2,500 Interest Deduction With a RAP $0 Payment, Multi-Servicer Transfers, and the Electronic Delivery Election

November 2026 is the pre-tax-season window to verify your 1098-E setup before federal student loan servicers mail or post the 2026 interest statement in late January 2027. For borrowers who switched from SAVE to RAP during 2026 (approximately 7.9 million affected borrowers), the form delivery is more complicated than in prior years: paid interest is split across the pre-RAP and post-RAP portions of the year, additional servicer transfers landed loans at two or three servicers during the calendar year, and the 50 percent RAP interest waiver generates a frequently-asked question about whether waived interest counts as deductible. It does not. This guide walks the four moving pieces that affect the 2026 tax return: the $2,500 annual cap and the 2026 MAGI phase-outs, the paid-versus-waived interest distinction under 26 USC 221, how multi-servicer transfers split the form across two or three statements, and the late-November electronic-delivery consent deadline that most major servicers enforce internally before the January statement run.

Most borrower-focused 1098-E guidance treats the form as a passive mailer that arrives in late January and gets handed to the tax preparer. In a normal year that framing is adequate. 2026 is not a normal year for federal student loan borrowers. The SAVE-to-RAP transition that began on July 1, 2026 moved roughly 7.9 million borrowers off the SAVE plan onto RAP or IBR, in many cases with a mid-year servicer transfer attached. The RAP 50 percent interest waiver, which took effect for most borrowers in September or October 2026 depending on the servicer, changes which interest counts as paid versus forgiven. And the 2026 MAGI phase-out ranges for the student loan interest deduction were adjusted for inflation but were not changed in structure by the One Big Beautiful Bill or the 2026 RISE final regulations. Verifying each piece now, in the six-week window before the January 2027 statement run, prevents a late-February scramble when a tax preparer asks why only two of three 1098-E forms are on file.

The single most effective action a borrower can take right now is to log in to every federal student loan servicer portal where they held a loan during any part of 2026 (not just the current servicer) and confirm three things: the address of record for paper mail is current, the electronic-delivery consent election for tax forms is affirmatively set to yes if a paperless workflow is desired, and the year-to-date paid-interest figure under the account history matches the borrower's own records. Discrepancies at this step are easier to resolve now than after the 1098-E is generated and locked for the IRS filing cycle. The $2,500 Cap and the 2026 MAGI Phase-Out Ranges

The student loan interest deduction under 26 USC 221 permits an above-the-line deduction (adjustment to income on Schedule 1 line 21 of Form 1040, not an itemized deduction) of up to $2,500 per year of qualified student loan interest paid. The deduction is available whether the borrower itemizes or takes the standard deduction, which makes it one of the few tax benefits that remains useful post-TCJA for borrowers who do not itemize. The cap is a hard dollar amount that has not been indexed for inflation since the statute was enacted; it is $2,500 for 2026 as it was for 2025 and 2024.

Eligibility phases out based on modified adjusted gross income (MAGI, which for most borrowers is AGI from Form 1040 line 11 plus a handful of add-backs that rarely apply). For the 2026 tax year, the IRS inflation-adjusted phase-out ranges are approximately $85,000 to $100,000 for single filers and $175,000 to $205,000 for married filing jointly. Within the phase-out range the deduction is reduced proportionally; above the top of the range it is zero. Married filing separately filers are not permitted to claim the deduction at any MAGI level, which is a specific rule that catches borrowers running the MFS strategy for a lower RAP payment. The trade-off is clean: a borrower who files MFS to shield a spouse's income from the RAP calculation loses the up to $2,500 federal interest deduction, worth up to $550 in federal tax at the 22 percent marginal rate. Whether the RAP monthly payment reduction from MFS exceeds $550 over the plan year is a borrower-specific calculation; compare the two filing paths with the Plan Comparison tool.

Paid Interest Versus the RAP 50% Interest Waiver

The RAP 50 percent interest waiver is the single most common source of 1098-E confusion for 2026 tax filers. Here is the mechanics in plain terms. Under the RAP plan, each monthly billing cycle begins with the servicer calculating the full accrued interest for the month. The servicer then zeroes out 50 percent of that accrued interest before any payment is applied, reducing the amount of interest that becomes owed by the borrower. The waived 50 percent is forgiven interest that never joined the outstanding balance; it is not paid interest. The remaining 50 percent of accrued interest sits on the balance, and when the borrower's monthly payment comes in, the payment is applied first to accrued interest and then to principal. The 50 percent RAP principal match (a separate feature that adds up to 50 percent of the principal portion of the payment as a government-funded principal contribution) also is not paid interest; it is a principal addition.

For 1098-E purposes, only the interest actually paid out of the borrower's (or employer's under IRC Section 127) cash makes it into box 1 of the form. The servicer excludes waived interest and principal-match contributions from the paid-interest total before issuing the statement. A RAP borrower with a $30 monthly payment where $18 applies to interest and $12 applies to principal in a given month will see $18 of that payment reported as paid interest in box 1, not $18 plus any waived amount. For a full RAP year at that rate, box 1 is approximately $216, well below the $2,500 annual cap. A borrower with a higher scheduled payment, or who made voluntary lump-sum payments, or who had an employer student-loan assistance program making payments on the borrower's behalf under IRC Section 127 (up to $5,250 per year tax-free through 2026), may have a materially higher box 1 figure.

Two specific borrower situations deserve attention. First, a borrower whose RAP monthly payment is $0 (income below the protected income threshold for family size, resulting in a zero scheduled payment) can still have paid interest in box 1 if the borrower made voluntary payments, consolidated a defaulted loan into RAP with a lump-sum payment, or had an IRC 127 employer program paying on their behalf. Second, a borrower with a positive scheduled RAP payment but whose employer student-loan benefit covered the entire payment in some months shows employer-paid interest in box 1 for those months; the employer's payment is reported as paid by the borrower for 1098-E purposes because the exclusion under IRC Section 127 operates at the income-inclusion level, not at the paid-interest level. Multi-Servicer Transfers in 2026: Why You Might Get Three 1098-E Forms

The SAVE-to-RAP transition that peaked between July and October 2026 involved not only a plan change but also, for a significant portion of borrowers, a servicer change. Loans that were held at EdFinancial under SAVE were, for many borrowers, transferred to Aidvantage or MOHELA upon RAP enrollment. A smaller number of borrowers experienced a second transfer in Q4 2026 as the Department of Education rebalanced portfolios across the federal loan servicer pool. Each transfer creates an additional 1098-E for the 2026 tax year because the IRS regulations under 26 CFR 1.6050S-2 require the holder of the loan at the time the interest is received to issue the form for the interest received during that holding period.

A representative borrower timeline: on January 1, 2026 the loan was at EdFinancial on SAVE. On May 15, 2026 the loan transferred to Aidvantage still on SAVE pending RAP enrollment. On July 1, 2026 the loan converted to RAP at Aidvantage. On September 20, 2026 the loan transferred to MOHELA on RAP. This borrower receives three 1098-E statements for 2026: one from EdFinancial covering January through May 14 interest, one from Aidvantage covering May 15 through September 19 interest, and one from MOHELA covering September 20 through December 31 interest. The borrower sums box 1 from all three forms up to the $2,500 annual cap and enters the total on Schedule 1 line 21 of Form 1040.

The practical risk is that a borrower who changed address or did not update the address of record at a prior servicer will not receive the paper 1098-E from that prior servicer; the electronic copy in the online portal may be the only accessible version. Prior servicers are required to maintain the 1098-E online for at least four years after the issuance year under IRS retention rules, but the online portal account may have been closed or deactivated after the loan transferred out. Call the prior servicer at the toll-free number for tax-form reissuance (listed on each servicer's tax information page) before January 20, 2027 to confirm the portal access remains open, or request a mailed copy to the current address. Keep a spreadsheet of all servicers that held any portion of the loan during 2026 and check each one off as the 1098-E is retrieved.

The Electronic Delivery Consent Deadline in Late November and December 2026

IRS regulations under 26 CFR 1.6050S-2 require an affirmative electronic-delivery consent from the borrower before a servicer may deliver the 1098-E only through the online portal rather than by paper mail. The consent must be made in a manner that reasonably demonstrates the borrower can access the electronic form (typically by making the consent itself electronic, which demonstrates the borrower has an internet connection and the required reader software). Each servicer manages the consent through a borrower-portal communication-preferences screen, and once given, the consent is effective for all future years until withdrawn.

Although the IRS regulations do not set a specific consent deadline before the January statement run, most major federal student loan servicers enforce an internal cutoff in the last business week of November 2026 or the first two weeks of December 2026 to allow operational time to set the paperless flag on the borrower's account before the January statement generation. A consent given after the internal cutoff may apply to the following year rather than the current year, which means the borrower receives a paper 1098-E for 2026 even though the online copy is also available. The paper mailing is not a problem (both versions report the same information), but a borrower who prefers paperless records to avoid losing the form in the physical mail should give consent before the end of November 2026.

For borrowers whose loans were transferred during 2026, the consent must be given separately at each servicer that received interest during the year. A consent given to the current servicer does not propagate to prior servicers, who issue their own 1098-E for the portion of the year they held the loan. This is the most commonly missed step. The borrower should list each servicer that held the loan during 2026 and log in to each portal to verify or set the electronic-delivery consent. Paper mail from a prior servicer may go to an outdated address, especially if the borrower moved during 2026.

The Under-$600 Edge Case: When No Form Is Mailed

Servicers are required to issue a 1098-E only when the borrower paid $600 or more in student loan interest during the calendar year. Below that threshold the servicer may issue a form voluntarily but is not required to. Many RAP borrowers with low scheduled payments will fall below $600 in 2026 paid interest and will not receive a form by default. The deduction remains claimable up to the actual interest paid and up to the $2,500 annual cap; the under-$600 rule is a servicer-reporting threshold, not a borrower-deduction threshold.

To claim the deduction without a 1098-E, the borrower reconstructs the interest-paid figure from the servicer's online payment history. Each posted monthly payment breaks into principal, interest, and (in rare cases) fees; the servicer portal under account history or payment history displays the breakdown. Sum the interest portion of each posted payment across all months in 2026 to get the annual total. For a RAP borrower with a $20 monthly payment where each payment applies approximately $14 to interest and $6 to principal, the annual interest paid is about $168, which is deductible subject to MAGI phase-out even though no form is issued. Export or screenshot the year-end payment history as backup documentation to retain for at least three years after the 2026 return is filed, in case the IRS sends a correspondence audit asking for documentation of the Schedule 1 line 21 number.

The Capitalized Interest Question

Capitalized interest is accrued interest that was added to the loan principal balance rather than paid. Under IRS guidance in Rev. Proc. 2005-50 and the subsequent 1098-E regulations, capitalized interest is treated as paid for purposes of the student loan interest deduction only when the borrower later pays down the capitalized amount through a loan payment. In other words, capitalization itself is not a deduction event; a later principal payment that reduces the capitalized balance is. Servicers generally do not break out which portion of a principal payment is retiring previously capitalized interest versus original principal, so the capitalized-interest deduction is typically claimed only when the loan is fully paid off or refinanced and the capitalized amount is unambiguously reduced to zero.

For 2026 specifically, the SAVE forbearance that ended in September 2026 did not capitalize interest at the end of the forbearance under the special rule published in the 2026 RISE regulations; that waiver applied to borrowers who moved from SAVE to RAP or IBR before December 31, 2026. Borrowers who switched to SAVE auto-enrolled standard plan in October 2026 instead faced a capitalization event, which may show as a one-time increase in principal on the loan detail page. The November-to-March Timeline Checklist

Week of November 23 to November 29, 2026: List every federal student loan servicer that held your loan during any part of 2026 (check NSLDS at studentaid.gov under your account's loan servicer history if unsure). For each servicer, log in to the borrower portal and confirm address of record, phone number, and email on file are current. Set or verify electronic-delivery consent for tax forms in communication preferences.

Week of November 30 to December 6, 2026: Pull the year-to-date payment history from each servicer portal. Export or screenshot each one for your records. Sum the interest portion of each payment to estimate your 2026 paid-interest figure. Compare against the $2,500 annual cap and the 2026 MAGI phase-out range for your filing status.

Week of December 7 to December 20, 2026: If an employer student-loan assistance program made payments on your behalf during 2026 under IRC Section 127, request a year-end payment summary from the employer benefits team. Confirm the total employer-paid interest is included in your paid-interest estimate.

Week of January 20 to January 31, 2027: 1098-E forms post to each servicer's online portal by January 31, 2027. For paper mail recipients, forms arrive at the address of record by approximately January 31, 2027. Verify every form is received. If any are missing, call the issuing servicer's tax information toll-free line for a reissue.

February 1 to February 15, 2027: Sum box 1 across all 1098-E forms (up to the $2,500 cap). Enter the total on Schedule 1 line 21 of Form 1040. If MAGI falls within the phase-out range, apply the proportional reduction per the Form 1040 instructions. For borrowers running MFS, the deduction is not available regardless of MAGI; confirm the RAP payment savings from MFS exceed the forgone deduction value.

March 1 to April 15, 2027: If a 1098-E is still missing after a reissue request, file the return claiming the deduction based on your own payment history reconstruction and attach a written explanation. The IRS accepts reasonable reconstruction when the servicer's reporting is late or incomplete, provided the borrower's records support the claimed figure.

The Bottom Line

November 2026 is the right moment to verify 1098-E setup across every federal student loan servicer that held your loan during any part of 2026. Set electronic-delivery consent before the end of November at each servicer, confirm the address of record for paper mail, pull year-to-date payment history for backup documentation, and estimate your 2026 paid-interest figure against the $2,500 annual cap and the approximately $85,000 to $100,000 single filer MAGI phase-out range (approximately $175,000 to $205,000 for married filing jointly). The 50 percent RAP interest waiver reduces the paid-interest number because waived interest was never paid; the servicer handles the exclusion in box 1 of the form so no borrower adjustment is required. Multi-servicer transfers during 2026 produce two or three separate 1098-E statements that must all be summed at tax time; missing any one understates the deduction. For borrowers under the $600 reporting threshold who do not receive a form automatically, the deduction is still claimable from reconstructed payment history.
