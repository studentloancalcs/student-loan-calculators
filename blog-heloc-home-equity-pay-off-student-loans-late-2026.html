Using HELOC or Home Equity to Pay Off Student Loans in Late 2026: The Math on 7.36% HELOC vs 6.52% Federal Rates, Why It's Usually a Mistake, and the Narrow Case Where It Works

With federal student loan payments restarted on October 1 and homeowners sitting on record home equity, a tempting question is making the rounds in financial forums: should I tap my HELOC or take a home equity loan to retire my student debt and be done with it? The headline math looks seductive. The real math is more complicated. This article works through the current rate spread, the five federal protections that disappear the moment the balance moves to a home equity product, the IRS deductibility question that catches most borrowers by surprise, three worked examples at $25,000, $60,000, and $120,000 balances, and the one narrow case where tapping home equity actually works.

The idea has been around for decades: borrow against the house, retire the student debt, consolidate everything into one monthly payment at a potentially lower rate. In 2008 and again in 2015, personal finance sites regularly recommended it. In October 2026, the recommendation has quietly disappeared from most mainstream outlets, and for good reason. The rate spread is thinner than it looks, the federal protections you surrender are structurally more valuable than most borrowers realize, and the one narrow scenario where the move works has been narrowed further by the 2026 Repayment Assistance Plan rulebook and the end of the SAVE plan transition. Below is the field guide you would hand a homeowner-borrower seriously considering the move.

The Rate Spread as of October 28, 2026

The Bankrate national average HELOC rate for a standard $30,000 line (700 FICO, 80 percent combined loan-to-value ratio, primary single-family home) was 7.36 percent as of October 28, 2026, with a range of 3.99 to 11.85 percent across lenders and borrower profiles. Fixed-rate home equity loans run roughly 15 to 50 basis points higher than variable HELOCs for the same borrower; call it 7.5 to 7.9 percent as a working national average for a thirty thousand dollar fixed home equity loan at the end of October 2026.

On the federal student loan side, the rates for loans first disbursed between July 1, 2026 and June 30, 2027 are 6.52 percent for Direct Subsidized and Undergraduate Unsubsidized Loans, 8.07 percent for Graduate Unsubsidized Loans, and 9.07 percent for Direct PLUS Loans (both Parent PLUS and Grad PLUS). These are fixed for the life of the loan. Borrowers with older federal loans have mixed portfolios: undergraduate loans originated during the 2020-21 academic year carry a 2.75 percent rate; loans from 2021-22 at 3.73 percent; loans from 2023-24 at 5.5 percent. Grad PLUS from the same vintages ranges from 5.3 percent (2020-21) to 7.9 percent (2023-24).

The naive spread calculation: a borrower with 9.07 percent current-vintage Grad PLUS could move the balance to a 7.36 percent HELOC and save 171 basis points. On a $45,000 balance over a 10-year payoff, that is roughly $4,500 in nominal interest savings. The problem is that the comparison treats one fixed-rate loan with federal protections against one variable-rate loan with none of them. A fair comparison must account for the variable rate risk, the protections surrendered, the IRS deductibility difference, and (for borrowers heading into retirement) the loss of federal death and disability discharge.

The Variable Rate Problem

A HELOC rate is almost always variable, tied to the Prime Rate plus a lender margin that stays fixed. Prime Rate changes with the Federal Reserve's federal funds target rate; when the Fed raises or cuts, the HELOC payment adjusts on the following billing cycle. The 7.36 percent average rate in late October 2026 reflects a Fed federal funds target of roughly 4.00 to 4.25 percent (down from the 2023 peak of 5.25 to 5.50 percent). If the Fed cuts another 100 basis points over the next twelve months, the HELOC rate drops to roughly 6.36 percent. If instead inflation re-accelerates and the Fed holds or hikes, the HELOC rate stays at 7.36 or moves to 8 percent or higher.

The federal student loan rate is frozen for the life of the loan. A borrower who paid off a 9.07 percent Grad PLUS with a 7.36 percent HELOC in October 2026 and then watched the HELOC rate climb to 9 percent in 2028 is now paying a higher rate than they would have on the federal loan, and the balance is now secured by their home instead of being a federal obligation. Fixed-rate home equity loans eliminate this risk at the cost of a slightly higher starting rate; borrowers seriously pursuing the home-equity-for-student-loans path should price fixed home equity loans, not variable HELOCs, to make the comparison honest.

The Five Federal Protections You Surrender

1. Income-driven repayment. Federal student loans can enroll in the Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP), Income-Based Repayment (IBR), or the tiered standard plan. During an income drop, RAP's early recertification rule drops the monthly payment to the $10 minimum within one to two billing cycles. The 50 percent principal match and interest waiver during RAP mean a borrower paying $10 per month during a leave or job loss is still retiring $5 of principal per month and accruing no new interest. HELOCs offer none of this. The amortization schedule is contractual; a job loss means a missed payment, a notice of default, and in the worst case a foreclosure on the home. See our RAP Calculator for the exact monthly payment on a federal loan at any AGI and family size.

2. Public Service Loan Forgiveness. 120 qualifying monthly payments on federal Direct Loans while employed at a 501(c)(3) nonprofit, government agency, or AmeriCorps-eligible employer forgive the remaining balance tax-free. The 2026 PSLF rulebook has tightened some employer eligibility language (see our PSLF employer eligibility update) but the core benefit is unchanged. A borrower on month 95 of PSLF who moves their balance to a HELOC loses the remaining 25 months of credit and the forgiveness that would have followed. For a nurse at a nonprofit hospital with a $62,000 balance paying $185 per month under RAP, forgiveness at month 120 produces roughly $55,000 of tax-free debt cancellation. A HELOC payoff at month 95 destroys that value. Our PSLF Tracker models the exact forgiveness amount at any month of qualifying payment.

3. Death and disability discharge. Federal Direct Loans are discharged tax-free on the borrower's death and on a Total and Permanent Disability (TPD) determination. The discharge is automatic for Direct Loans (the servicer processes it from a death certificate or TPD determination letter). A HELOC is a lien on the home that survives the borrower's death. The surviving spouse or estate must either refinance the lien, sell the home, or default and face foreclosure. For a sixty-year-old parent considering a HELOC to retire a $40,000 Parent PLUS balance, the death discharge value alone (if the parent reaches average life expectancy with the balance still outstanding) can run $5,000 to $15,000 in present-value terms, which closes most of the apparent rate advantage.

4. Deferment and forbearance. Federal borrowers have statutory rights to pause payments during unemployment (up to 36 cumulative months of unemployment deferment), cancer treatment (special cancer deferment, unlimited duration during active treatment plus six months after), military service (USERRA and SCRA protections), and economic hardship (up to 36 months of hardship deferment, income-based criteria). HELOCs offer no statutory parallel. Some HELOC servicers offer short discretionary forbearance during hardship; most do not, and nothing requires them to.

5. Treasury Offset floor for Social Security. Social Security retirement and disability benefits are protected against Treasury Offset for defaulted federal student loans to a $750 monthly floor (codified in the 2024 Protecting Older Americans Act). A HELOC lien has no comparable protection; foreclosure on a primary residence to satisfy a HELOC can proceed regardless of the borrower's age or Social Security status, subject only to state-specific homestead protections that in most states do not shield a home from the holder of a voluntary consensual lien like a HELOC.

The IRS Deductibility Question

Under the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (which remains in effect for tax year 2026), home equity interest is deductible only when the proceeds are used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home that secures the loan. Using HELOC proceeds to pay off student loans is explicitly a non-qualifying use; the interest paid on that portion of the HELOC balance is personal interest, not deductible on Schedule A. Many borrowers (and some loan officers) are still operating on the pre-2018 rule, under which home equity interest was deductible for most uses up to $100,000 of loan balance. That rule has been gone for nearly nine years and the TCJA sunset provisions do not restore it for 2026.

On the federal student loan side, the $2,500 above-the-line student loan interest deduction (phased out at modified AGI between $85,000 and $100,000 single, and $175,000 and $205,000 married filing jointly for 2026) remains fully available. The deduction is available even for taxpayers who take the standard deduction (which is $16,100 single, $32,200 married filing jointly, and $24,150 head of household in 2026). For a borrower in the 22 percent federal bracket, the full $2,500 deduction is worth $550 per year. Over the ten-year payoff of a typical federal balance, that is $5,500 of foregone tax savings on the HELOC side of the comparison, which further erodes the apparent rate spread.

The RAP interest waiver complicates the deduction math further for current RAP enrollees. Under RAP's interest waiver, accrued interest beyond what the monthly payment covers is zeroed out each month; the borrower never actually pays that interest, so there is nothing to deduct. For a borrower at $10 per month RAP, the student loan interest deduction is effectively $120 per year, not $2,500. For higher-income RAP borrowers paying $300 to $600 per month, the deduction tracks the actual interest paid, which is still often below $2,500. The deduction is more valuable for borrowers on the tiered standard plan or on the extended plan where the full interest is being paid. See our RAP interest deduction guide for the exact math.

Three Worked Examples

Example 1: $25,000 undergraduate balance, 5.5 percent rate (2023-24 vintage). Monthly federal payment on a 10-year standard plan is roughly $271. HELOC at 7.36 percent for the same ten years runs roughly $294 per month, which is higher despite the "lower" headline rate because the HELOC starts at the higher rate while this borrower's older federal loan is at 5.5 percent. The borrower pays an extra $23 per month and loses all five federal protections. Total extra interest paid over ten years: $2,760. The move is pure wealth destruction. Even a 5.5 percent fixed home equity loan would roughly break even on rate while still eliminating the protections. For this borrower, the federal payoff calculator shows accelerated principal payments (an extra $100 per month on the standard federal plan) pay off the loan 2.5 years early with none of the HELOC risks.

Example 2: $60,000 Grad PLUS balance, 9.07 percent rate (2026-27 vintage). Monthly federal payment on the 10-year standard plan is roughly $763. HELOC at 7.36 percent for the same term runs roughly $706 per month, saving $57 per month and $6,840 over ten years. On paper this is the strongest HELOC-wins scenario in the current rate environment. In practice, the Grad PLUS borrower who is on a PSLF track at a nonprofit hospital, a research university, or a government lab would be giving up tax-free forgiveness of roughly $50,000 to $60,000 by paying the balance off instead of letting PSLF retire it. Even for a Grad PLUS borrower not on PSLF, enrolling in RAP produces monthly payments far below $763 for most income levels; the HELOC payment is contractually fixed while the RAP payment adjusts with income. The HELOC wins the pure rate math and loses on nearly every other dimension. See the plan comparison tool to model this borrower's exact RAP, IBR, and standard plan payments.

Example 3: $120,000 mixed portfolio (undergraduate $40K at 4.5 percent, graduate $80K at 7.5 percent, mixed vintages). Blended federal rate: roughly 6.5 percent. HELOC at 7.36 percent is above the blended federal rate; the home equity move loses on rate before accounting for protections. The borrower who refinances only the graduate portion into a fixed home equity loan at 7.5 percent and keeps the undergraduate portion on federal plans does slightly better: they capture the stability of the fixed rate for the higher-rate portion and preserve federal protections for the undergraduate portion. This is the closest to a case where partial home equity payoff makes sense, and it still works only if the borrower is not pursuing PSLF, has substantial liquid reserves, and does not expect income volatility.

The One Narrow Scenario Where It Works

Four conditions must all hold for a home equity payoff to beat the federal path:

(1) The federal loan rate is substantially above the current fixed home equity loan rate. This mostly means Grad PLUS originated 2024 or later (8-9 percent federal) versus fixed home equity loans at 7-7.5 percent. Pre-2024 undergraduate loans at 2.75 to 5.5 percent never qualify. Parent PLUS at 9.07 percent qualifies on rate but typically fails condition 4 below.

(2) The borrower does not qualify for and will not pursue PSLF, RAP forgiveness, or the student loan interest deduction. High-income private-sector borrower, no plans to change employers, modified AGI above the deduction phaseout ceiling.

(3) Income is stable with low job-loss probability over the payoff period (five to ten years), and the borrower has six months of liquid reserves outside the home equity position.

(4) The borrower uses a fixed-rate home equity loan, not a variable HELOC, and is young enough that the federal death discharge is not a material consideration.

In practice, the person who passes all four tests is a 32-year-old tech worker with a $240,000 household income, $55,000 in Grad PLUS debt at 9.07 percent, no PSLF intent, strong home equity, and the ability to lock a 7.1 percent fixed home equity loan for ten years. For this person, the move saves roughly $7,000 to $10,000 in net present value interest. For roughly every other home-owning student loan borrower in late 2026, the federal path wins.

The Bottom Line

The HELOC-pays-off-student-loans idea trades a federal obligation with five layers of consumer protection, a tax-free forgiveness pathway, and a lower-of-two-outcomes income-driven payment floor for a home equity lien with a variable rate, no forgiveness, and the house as collateral. The rate spread in October 2026 is thin (171 basis points at the extreme favorable case, often zero or negative for pre-2024 loans) and the risk transfer is enormous. The payoff math works only in the specific case of a high-income, high-stability, non-PSLF borrower with recent Grad PLUS debt who uses a fixed-rate home equity loan rather than a variable HELOC. For nearly every other borrower, the honest answer is to stay with the federal system, enroll in the right income-driven plan, make the strategic AGI-reduction moves that lower the payment legitimately, and let PSLF or RAP forgiveness retire the balance over 10 to 25 years.

Before pricing a HELOC or home equity loan for a student loan payoff, run the exact numbers on your federal balance using the RAP Calculator, the plan comparison tool, and (if applicable) the PSLF Tracker. The federal path almost always wins the full comparison once protections, forgiveness value, tax deductibility, and income-driven floor adjustments are counted. The HELOC payoff that looks like a $5,000 win on the rate line is often a $25,000 loss on the full ledger.
