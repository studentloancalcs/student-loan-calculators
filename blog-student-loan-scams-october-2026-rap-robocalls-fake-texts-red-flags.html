Student Loan Scam Alert Late October 2026: The 7 Red Flags of RAP Robocalls, Fake Servicer Texts, and "Debt Relief" Fee Schemes Targeting Payment-Restart Borrowers

Three weeks into payment restart, state attorneys general and the Federal Trade Commission are reporting a surge of scam calls, texts, and emails aimed at the forty-six million federal student loan borrowers now making real monthly payments again. The schemes impersonate MOHELA, Nelnet, Aidvantage, and the Education Department; they promise RAP qualification in exchange for an upfront fee; they harvest Social Security numbers under the cover of fake “verification” forms. This article is the field guide: the seven red flags that mark every student loan scam, the four schemes most common in fall 2026, how to verify a real servicer message in under two minutes, and the exact steps to take if you already clicked a bad link.

The pattern is familiar from every past payment-restart event. The FTC first documented a scam surge after the 2023 payment restart; a second wave followed the Supreme Court's rejection of the Biden forgiveness plan; and the fall 2026 restart, which also includes the simultaneous launch of the Repayment Assistance Plan, is on pace to be the largest surge yet. Scammers prey on confusion, time pressure, and the simple fact that most borrowers have not interacted with their servicer in six years. The defense is not technical. It is a checklist, and this article is the checklist.

The Seven Red Flags of a Student Loan Scam

1. The message demands an immediate action under threat. “Your account will be sent to collections tomorrow unless you call back within one hour.” “Your RAP enrollment will be canceled if you do not click this link today.” Real servicer communications about delinquency, default, or plan changes come with a minimum of fifteen days of lead time, often thirty to ninety days, and arrive both in your account's message center and by mail. Any communication that creates an artificial one-hour, twenty-four-hour, or three-day panic is a scam.

2. The message asks for an upfront fee for a federal program. No federal program charges a fee to apply. Not RAP, not IBR, not PSLF, not consolidation, not deferment, not forbearance, not buyback, not death or disability discharge. Any company that says it will enroll you in a federal plan for a flat fee, a percentage, a monthly membership, or a “document preparation” fee is running an illegal scheme under the FTC's advance-fee rule for debt relief services.

3. The caller asks for your studentaid.gov FSA ID password. Nobody at your servicer, nobody at the Education Department, nobody in federal customer service ever needs your FSA ID password. If a “representative” asks for it, the goal is to log in as you, change your contact information, redirect your refund, or apply for a loan in your name. The password is yours; share it with no one, including anyone who says they need it to “verify your file” or “update your account.”

4. The payment method is gift card, wire, Zelle, Venmo, Cash App, or cryptocurrency. The federal government does not accept any of these. Your servicer accepts ACH debit from your bank, debit or credit card through the online portal (sometimes with a processing fee), or a mailed paper check. Treasury Offset collections on defaulted loans run through Treasury's Pay.gov system. Nothing else is legitimate.

5. The communication arrived through a channel you did not opt into. If you have never texted with your servicer and a text message appears, treat it as suspicious until proven otherwise. If you have never received a servicer phone call and one arrives, hang up and call back using the number on your statement. Scammers rely on borrowers' inability to distinguish an authentic first-time contact from a fraudulent one; the safe move is to assume any first-time outbound contact is fake and verify by inbound contact to a known number.

6. The sender email domain is subtly wrong. Real servicer domains include mohela.com, nelnet.com, aidvantage.com, edfinancial.com, and the Education Department's studentaid.gov. Scam domains add characters, swap letters, use the servicer name as a subdomain of a lookalike domain (mohela.customerupdate.com instead of mohela.com), or use .net, .org, .co, or .us variants of the servicer name. Check the full domain in the sender address before trusting any email.

7. The URL in the link does not match where it says it goes. On a desktop, hover over the link without clicking; the real destination appears in the status bar at the bottom of the browser. On mobile, long-press the link to see the destination. A link that reads “studentaid.gov/rap-enrollment” but points to bit.ly/3kj92pq or rap-enroll-portal.com is a phishing link. The destination domain, not the display text, decides whether the link is safe.

The Four Schemes Most Common in Fall 2026

Scheme 1: The Fake RAP Qualification Robocall

A recorded voice (sometimes an AI-generated voice) tells you that you have been pre-qualified for the Repayment Assistance Plan and that your monthly payment can be reduced to $0 if you “press 1 to speak with an enrollment specialist.” If you press 1, a live person asks for your Social Security number, your date of birth, your bank account, and a “processing fee” of $499 to $1,499. There is no enrollment, no file, no specialist. The RAP application is free at studentaid.gov. If you qualify for a $0 RAP payment, your servicer will tell you after you file the IDR application; no robocaller has the authority to pre-qualify you. For borrowers wondering what a real $0 RAP payment looks like, see our RAP Calculator, which runs the actual federal formula.

Scheme 2: The Fake MOHELA/Nelnet/Aidvantage Phishing Text

A text message appears, often from a number that looks like a toll-free line, telling you that your account is past due, that your autopay has failed, that your enrollment has lapsed, or that your refund is ready to be released. A short link is included. The link lands on a page that is a pixel-perfect copy of your servicer's login screen, except the domain is slightly off. If you enter your username and password, the scammer logs in as you, changes your mailing address and bank account, and intercepts any mailed documents or refund disbursements. The fix is simple: never log in through a text link. Open your browser and type the servicer's known URL by hand, or use a bookmark you set up during a session you know was legitimate.

Scheme 3: The Upfront-Fee Debt Relief Company

A professionally designed website appears high in Google search for terms like “RAP enrollment help,” “student loan forgiveness 2026,” or “PSLF application assistance.” The company offers to prepare and submit your IDR, PSLF, or consolidation paperwork for a fee of $299 to $1,699, sometimes recurring monthly as “account monitoring.” They may have an impressive logo, five-star reviews, and even a BBB rating. What they do not have is any authority to deal with the Education Department on your behalf; after you pay, they fill out the same free application you could have filed yourself at studentaid.gov, often with errors that get the application rejected. The FTC has sued dozens of these companies since 2015, and the advance-fee rule for federal student loan debt relief services makes the business model illegal. If you have already paid such a company, dispute the charge with your bank or card issuer within sixty days (the Fair Credit Billing Act window) and file a complaint at reportfraud.ftc.gov.

Scheme 4: The Social Security Number Harvesting Form

A website or email asks you to “verify your identity” or “confirm your enrollment eligibility” by submitting a form that asks for your Social Security number, date of birth, mother's maiden name, bank account numbers, and sometimes a driver's license scan. The form may be branded with the Department of Education seal or a servicer logo. The information goes into a database that is then used for tax refund fraud, new credit account openings, and resale on the dark web. No legitimate federal process asks for all of these pieces of information in a single unsolicited form. If a form is asking for everything at once, it is a harvesting operation. Close the tab.

How to Verify a Real Servicer Message in Under Two Minutes

Step 1. Do not click the link, do not reply to the text, do not call the number in the message, do not press any digit on the automated system. Set the message aside.

Step 2. Open a fresh browser tab and type your servicer's URL by hand or use a bookmark you trust. If you are not sure who your servicer is, log in to studentaid.gov and your Dashboard shows the current servicer for each loan.

Step 3. Log in with your known username and password and go to the secure message center. If the notice is real, it appears there. If the notice is important (payment due, delinquency, plan change, enrollment status), it is in the message center and often also in the account's “Correspondence” or “Documents” tab.

Step 4. If the message does not appear in your account, assume it was fake and delete it. If it does appear, read it inside your account; take any action from inside your account rather than from the external message.

Step 5. If you want to confirm by phone, call the servicer back at the number printed on your most recent statement or on studentaid.gov. Do not call the number in the suspicious message. Expect hold times of twenty to ninety minutes during peak hours in October and November 2026, and plan accordingly.

What a Real Fall 2026 Servicer Communication Looks Like

A real RAP enrollment confirmation arrives in your servicer's secure message center and sometimes also by mailed paper letter. It states your enrollment status, your first due date, your monthly payment amount, your recertification anniversary, and your 50 percent principal match and interest waiver status if applicable. For borrowers who want to confirm the math on their first bill, our first RAP bill math check guide walks through the exact formula and the two spots where servicers most often make errors. A real past-due notice includes a specific delinquency date, the dollar amount owed, and a cure window of at least fifteen days, and it never pressures you to pay by gift card or wire.

A real credit reporting notice tells you that late payments will be reported to the three credit bureaus after a specific delinquency date (typically ninety days after the missed due date for most loan types in the current reporting restart). It does not threaten immediate credit damage for an on-time account. For context on how the reporting restart works, see our federal credit reporting restart article.

If You Already Clicked, Shared, or Paid: The 24-Hour Response Plan

Hour 1 through hour 2. Log in to studentaid.gov from a device you trust (ideally not the one on which you clicked the suspicious link) and change your FSA ID password. Enable multi-factor authentication if you have not already. Repeat the password change on your servicer's website and on any other financial account that uses the same password. Scammers test leaked passwords against hundreds of other sites within hours.

Hour 2 through hour 6. Place a free credit freeze with all three credit bureaus online (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion). A freeze is more protective than a fraud alert and takes a few minutes per bureau. It prevents new credit from being opened in your name without your explicit unfreezing. The freeze is free, permanent until you lift it, and does not affect your credit score.

Hour 6 through hour 24. If you shared your Social Security number, request an IRS Identity Protection PIN at irs.gov/getanippin; this prevents a fraudulent tax return from being filed in your name. File a report at IdentityTheft.gov to create a federal recovery plan tailored to what you shared. If you paid money, dispute the charge with your bank or card issuer under the Fair Credit Billing Act; most issuers will reverse the charge if reported within sixty days.

Within the first week. Pull all three free credit reports at annualcreditreport.com and look for new accounts, address changes, or unauthorized inquiries. Report the specific scam to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov, to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau at consumerfinance.gov/complaint, to the Education Department Office of Inspector General at oig.ed.gov, and to your state attorney general's consumer protection office. Each agency's complaint feeds a different enforcement database and improves the odds of civil or criminal action against the scammer.

Reporting Resources

Federal Trade Commission: reportfraud.ftc.gov. The FTC uses complaints to build cases against repeat offenders and feeds the data to law enforcement nationwide.

Consumer Financial Protection Bureau: consumerfinance.gov/complaint. The CFPB specifically handles complaints about student loan servicers and debt relief companies, including the servicers that may be the subject of impersonation.

Education Department Office of Inspector General: oig.ed.gov/report-fraud. The OIG handles fraud involving federal student aid programs, including impersonation of federal officials.

Internet Crime Complaint Center: ic3.gov. The IC3 (an FBI partnership) handles cyber-enabled fraud and funnels complaints to field offices.

State Attorney General: Each state has a consumer protection division; most are reachable through [state].gov/consumer or ag.[state].gov. State attorneys general have broader civil enforcement powers than federal agencies in many cases.

Credit Bureaus for freezes and fraud alerts: Equifax (equifax.com/credit-report-services), Experian (experian.com/freeze), TransUnion (transunion.com/credit-freeze). Freezes are free and online.

The Bottom Line

Payment restart on October 1 flipped a switch for scammers. The schemes are familiar but the scale is new, and the overlap with RAP enrollment confusion gives the schemes extra traction. The defense is a short discipline: no upfront fees for federal programs, no gift-card or wire payments, no clicking a link in an unsolicited text, no sharing of FSA ID passwords, and no reciting of Social Security numbers to inbound callers. When in doubt, log in to the servicer directly through a known URL and verify the message in the account's secure message center.

If you have free time this week, two preventive moves pay for themselves. First, freeze your credit at all three bureaus; it blocks the most common post-breach fraud. Second, save your servicer's phone number from your most recent statement into your phone contacts under the servicer's full official name, so you can verify any future inbound call against that stored number. Both steps take under twenty minutes combined and remove ninety percent of the attack surface scammers rely on.
