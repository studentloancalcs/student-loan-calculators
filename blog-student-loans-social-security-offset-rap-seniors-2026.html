Student Loans and Social Security October 2026: The 15% Benefit Offset Restart, the $750 Monthly Protection Floor, and How RAP Enrollment Rescues Senior Borrowers Facing Garnishment

Treasury Offset of Social Security benefits for defaulted federal student loans quietly restarted this year, and roughly 450,000 Social Security recipients are now in the sixty-five-day pre-offset notice window or already seeing a reduced benefit deposit. This article is the field guide for the retirees, surviving spouses, and disabled borrowers caught in that window: how the 15 percent offset works, how the $750 statutory floor protects a core slice of each benefit, how a hardship exemption gets filed, and how Loan Rehabilitation plus RAP enrollment can take a borrower from a reduced check back to a full benefit in nine months.

Older Americans with student loan debt are an unusually invisible population. They are a small share of federal borrowers (about 7.3 percent are over sixty-two according to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's 2026 analysis), but their defaults have outsized consequences: a Social Security check that was already modest gets cut by fifteen percent, and the gap between a $1,980 average benefit and a $1,683 offset-reduced benefit is often the gap between paying for prescription drugs and skipping doses. Student loan debt over age sixty-two is a different problem from student loan debt at thirty, and the collection restart in 2026 is the first time the federal government has resumed Social Security offset at scale since 2020.

How Treasury Offset of Social Security Benefits Works

The legal authority for garnishing Social Security benefits to pay a defaulted federal student loan sits in 31 U.S.C. 3716, the Debt Collection Improvement Act of 1996. Under the Treasury Offset Program (TOP), the Treasury Department withholds a portion of certain federal payments owed to a borrower in default on a federal debt and routes the withheld amount to the agency owed. For federal student loans, the referring agency is the Education Department, and the withholding rate for Social Security Title II benefits (retirement, survivor, and disability insurance) is 15 percent of the monthly benefit above the statutory protection floor. Supplemental Security Income (SSI) is specifically exempt from offset by statute; only Title II benefits are offsettable.

The sequence is familiar: a borrower defaults on a federal student loan (defined as 270 days delinquent for most loan types), the Education Department refers the debt to Treasury, Treasury sends a sixty-five-day notice to the borrower at the address of record, the window elapses without the borrower taking action, and the next monthly Social Security deposit is reduced by the offset amount. The reduced deposit continues each month until the default is resolved or a hardship exemption is granted. During the 2020 through 2025 collections pause, no new offsets were started; the formal restart notice went out in early 2026 and the first offsets of the restart cycle hit September 2026 benefit deposits.

The $750 Monthly Protection Floor

Before 2024, the Social Security offset floor was $750 per month ($9,000 per year) by regulation, but the figure had not been adjusted since 1996 and advocates argued that a figure set at the 1996 federal poverty line of about $650 per month had lost roughly half its real value over three decades. The Protecting Older Americans from Garnishment of Social Security Benefits Act of 2024 codified the $750 floor in statute (so Treasury could not raise it through regulation but Congress could raise it by statute), and the floor applies to all offsets occurring in 2025 and later. The current floor is still $750 per month, not indexed to inflation, which means the real value of the protection continues to erode each year.

The arithmetic is straightforward. For a benefit of $B per month and the floor at $750, the offset is calculated as the lesser of 15 percent of $B or 15 percent of ($B minus $750). The second formula applies in practice because the first is only lower when the benefit is extraordinary. For the 2026 average Social Security retirement benefit of $1,980 per month, the offset is 15 percent of ($1,980 minus $750), or $184.50. For a benefit of $1,250 per month, the offset is 15 percent of ($1,250 minus $750), or $75. For a benefit of $750 or less per month, the offset is $0; the entire benefit is protected. This structure is important because it means the lowest-benefit retirees, who often have the greatest need, are entirely shielded by the floor.

Five Paths to Stop Offset Within the 65-Day Window

Path 1: Loan Rehabilitation

The most-used path for seniors. Call the Education Department's Default Resolution Group at 1-800-621-3115 and ask to start a Rehabilitation Agreement. The agreement sets a monthly payment at fifteen percent of discretionary income with a $5 per month floor; for most Social Security-only households the payment is $5. Make nine on-time payments across ten consecutive months and the loan is formally removed from default. During the rehabilitation period, the Education Department requests that Treasury suspend offset; the suspension takes effect within one to two benefit cycles. After the ninth payment, the loan transfers to a non-default servicer and the borrower can enroll in RAP immediately. The rehabilitation is a one-time opportunity for each loan; a loan that defaults again after rehabilitation cannot be rehabilitated a second time, though it can still be consolidated.

Path 2: Direct Consolidation

Faster than rehabilitation, but with trade-offs. Log in to studentaid.gov and apply for a Direct Consolidation Loan; defaulted loans can be consolidated as long as the borrower agrees to repay the new consolidation loan under an income-driven plan (RAP, IBR, or the tiered standard plan) or makes three satisfactory voluntary payments on the defaulted loan before consolidation. The consolidation itself takes thirty to sixty days to process, after which the new loan is current (not in default) and the borrower immediately qualifies to apply for a $10 RAP payment. The trade-off: consolidation does not remove the default notation from the borrower's credit report in the same way rehabilitation does; the historical default stays on the credit report for seven years from the original default date. For most seniors who do not plan to apply for new credit, this trade-off is acceptable in exchange for the speed advantage.

Path 3: Financial Hardship Exemption

Request Form OTR-5 (or the equivalent current form) from the Education Department's Default Resolution Group. The form asks for monthly income, housing costs, utilities, food, transportation, medical and prescription drug costs, insurance, and other necessary expenses. If necessary monthly expenses after offset would exceed income, the Education Department can grant a Financial Hardship Exemption that suspends offset for a renewable twelve-month period. Supporting documents should include three months of bank statements, a current Social Security benefit statement, Medicare and Medigap premium statements, pharmacy receipts, utility bills, a lease or mortgage statement, and any medical bills in collections. The hardship exemption suspends offset but does not resolve the underlying default; most borrowers who use this path combine it with a rehabilitation or consolidation process so the debt itself gets fixed before the exemption expires.

Path 4: Compromise or Settlement

The Education Department has settlement authority on defaulted federal student loans in specific circumstances: the balance is small relative to the age and health of the borrower, the collection cost would exceed the recovery, or the borrower has limited remaining life expectancy and limited assets. Settlements come in three standard forms (waiver of collection costs, waiver of accrued interest, or a reduced principal payoff), and are not available on request but can be requested; the Default Resolution Group evaluates each case. Settlement is more realistic for seniors with limited life expectancy and documented inability to pay than for other defaulted borrowers.

Path 5: Treasury Offset Hearing

If you believe the debt is wrong, you have never seen the loan, the loan was discharged (death of the student in a Parent PLUS, Total and Permanent Disability of the parent borrower, closed school discharge, borrower defense), or you were a victim of identity theft, you can request a Treasury Offset hearing within the sixty-five-day notice window. The hearing is paper-based unless you specifically request an in-person or telephone hearing. If the hearing finds the debt is not valid, offset is stopped and any amounts already withheld in error are refunded.

How RAP Produces a $10 Payment for Social Security-Only Households

After rehabilitation or consolidation moves a loan out of default, enrolling in the Repayment Assistance Plan is where the long-term fix happens. The RAP formula uses Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) and household size, not gross benefit income. Under the IRS Social Security benefit taxation rules, up to 85 percent of a Social Security benefit is taxable, but the actual share depends on provisional income (half of Social Security plus other income). For a retiree whose only income is Social Security retirement, the taxable portion is often zero because provisional income is below the $25,000 (single) or $32,000 (married filing jointly) threshold. That retiree's AGI shows $0 for Social Security on the tax return. If the household also has a small pension or IRA distribution, some Social Security may become taxable, but AGI still tends to be modest.

RAP's two-tier formula says that a borrower with AGI at or below the Protected Income threshold (roughly 150 percent of the Federal Poverty Line, adjusted for household size) owes the $10 monthly minimum. For a single borrower in 2026 that threshold is 150 percent of $15,650 or $23,475; for a couple it is 150 percent of $21,230 or $31,845. A Social Security-only household almost always lands under this threshold, so the RAP payment is the $10 minimum. The 50 percent principal match credits $5 of each $10 payment toward principal, and the interest waiver zeroes out the remaining accrued interest for the month if the $10 does not cover the full month's interest. Over twenty-five years (the RAP forgiveness term), the borrower pays $3,000 in total payments and the remaining balance is forgiven as a tax-free event under current tax rules through 2025 and extended by rule through 2026. If the borrower qualifies for PSLF through a non-profit role (less common at Social Security age but not unheard of), forgiveness arrives after 120 payments.

A Worked Example: $25,000 Balance, $1,980 Social Security, Default Since 2019

Consider a 71-year-old retired borrower with a $25,000 federal student loan balance (originally $15,000, plus accrued interest and collection fees), who defaulted in 2019 and whose only income is a $1,980 monthly Social Security retirement benefit. The collections pause suspended offset from March 2020 through 2025. In August 2026 the sixty-five-day notice arrived; in September 2026 the first offset of $184.50 reduced the deposit to $1,795.50. The borrower calls the Default Resolution Group on day forty-five of the sixty-five-day window (though offset has already started; rehabilitation applications are accepted after offset starts, which only speeds up the suspension).

The borrower starts Loan Rehabilitation at $5 per month. Within one benefit cycle (one to two months) Treasury suspends the offset and the full $1,980 benefit resumes. After the ninth $5 payment (month ten of the rehabilitation period), the loan transfers to a non-default servicer. The borrower applies for RAP immediately and the servicer sets the monthly payment at the $10 RAP minimum. Over the next twenty-five years, the borrower pays $10 per month ($3,000 total), the 50 percent principal match credits $1,500 toward principal, the interest waiver zeroes out the remaining interest each month, and after 300 payments the remaining balance is forgiven. The net cost of the rehabilitation plus RAP path is $45 for rehabilitation ($5 times nine payments) plus $3,000 for RAP, or $3,045 over twenty-five years. Compared to the original path of fifteen percent offset on $1,980 for twenty-five years (which would total $55,350 in offsets over the same period), the rehabilitation plus RAP path saves $52,305.

Documentation to Gather Before Calling the Default Resolution Group

The first phone call to 1-800-621-3115 goes faster if the documents are ready. Before dialing, collect: the Social Security benefit award letter or most recent SSA-1099 (shows benefit amount), the Treasury Offset notice (shows loan amount and offset start date), the Education Department debt summary from studentaid.gov (log in to see current servicer and balance by loan), a bank statement showing the current month's Social Security deposit, and a list of monthly expenses if a hardship exemption is on the table. If the loan is a Parent PLUS and the student has died, bring the death certificate; if the loan was taken for a student who became permanently disabled and the parent is also disabled, bring the parent's TPD determination. For borrower defense claims, bring enrollment and attendance records from the school. For identity theft claims, bring a police report and an IdentityTheft.gov recovery plan.

What Not to Do

Do not pay a company to rehabilitate your loan. Rehabilitation is free at the Default Resolution Group. The seven red flags from our October 2026 scam alert apply with special force to seniors: unsolicited calls promising "pre-qualification" for a program, upfront "processing fees," requests for Social Security numbers or FSA ID passwords, and payment demands by gift card are all scams.

Do not ignore the sixty-five-day notice. Offset starts automatically at the end of the window. The suspension that comes with rehabilitation is faster if the application is in motion before the deadline, but even after offset starts, starting rehabilitation suspends it within one or two benefit cycles.

Do not borrow from a credit card, a reverse mortgage, or a retirement account to pay off the defaulted loan in a lump sum unless a licensed financial planner has reviewed the trade-off. For most seniors the RAP path (rehabilitate, then enroll in RAP at $10 per month, then let the twenty-five-year forgiveness clock run) is dramatically cheaper than any lump-sum payoff financed by other debt or by reduced retirement savings.

Do not assume Supplemental Security Income (SSI) is at risk. SSI is a separate program (Title XVI) and is exempt from Treasury Offset by statute. Only Title II benefits (retirement, survivor, disability insurance) can be offset.

Free Help Resources for Senior Borrowers

Education Department Default Resolution Group: 1-800-621-3115. The first stop for rehabilitation, consolidation, hardship exemption, and discharge requests. Wait times during the restart period average thirty to ninety minutes in October 2026; morning calls (before 10 a.m. Eastern) and midweek calls tend to be shortest.

Consumer Financial Protection Bureau: consumerfinance.gov/complaint and consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/student-loans. The CFPB specifically handles student loan servicing complaints and has a dedicated older-Americans team.

Legal Aid for the Elderly: Many state and county legal aid offices have specific programs for older Americans and will represent eligible seniors in default disputes and discharge applications at no charge. The National Consumer Law Center maintains a directory at nclc.org/for-consumers.

State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP): shiphelp.org or 1-877-839-2675. Not student-loan-specific but useful for seniors who need to understand how a student loan payment interacts with Medicare drug plan Extra Help or Medicaid eligibility.

AARP: aarp.org/money/credit-loans-debt/student-loans. General guidance and advocacy for older-borrower issues; AARP has lobbied for the $750 floor's inflation indexing.

The Bottom Line

For the 450,000 Social Security recipients caught in the fall 2026 offset restart, the pathway back to a full benefit is clear and well-worn: call the Default Resolution Group, start rehabilitation at $5 per month, let the offset suspend within one or two benefit cycles, make nine payments across ten months, and enroll in RAP at the $10 monthly minimum. The total out-of-pocket cost is roughly $3,045 over twenty-five years. The alternative, letting the 15 percent offset run its course, costs tens of thousands of dollars and reduces the monthly benefit for the rest of the borrower's life or until the loan is paid in full.

The $750 floor protects a base amount but does not solve the default. Even a benefit of $1,000 per month loses $37.50 to offset until the loan comes out of default, and the smaller the benefit, the greater the proportional impact of that $37.50. Resolving the default is almost always better than relying on the floor alone.
