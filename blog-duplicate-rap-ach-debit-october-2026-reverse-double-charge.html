Student Loan Payment Debited Twice in October 2026? The SAVE-to-RAP Autopay Overlap That's Double-Charging Borrowers — and How to Reverse It

An estimated 180,000 to 240,000 federal borrowers checked their bank apps this week to find two matching ACH debits from the same servicer, three days apart, on the same October statement. The cause is a residual SAVE autopay authorization that did not fully release when accounts converted to RAP. Here is why it is happening, which debit to reverse, and the two refund paths with their federal deadlines.

The SAVE-to-RAP transition that closed on September 30, 2026 moved roughly 7.7 million federal borrowers from the shuttered SAVE plan into the new Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) or, less often, into IBR. Most of the transition went smoothly. The autopay piece did not. For borrowers who had enabled autopay inside the old SAVE portal, the servicer created a new RAP autopay authorization but, in a subset of cases, failed to retire the old SAVE authorization in the ACH origination queue. When the October due date arrived, both authorizations fired. Two debits, same amount, same account, two to three business days apart.

The volume is small as a share of all borrowers (roughly 2.3 to 3.1 percent of autopay-enabled accounts, by the CFPB complaint tracker's October 7 estimate) but concentrated: borrowers at MOHELA, Nelnet, and Aidvantage are reporting it in near-equal numbers, and the duplicates are most common on accounts that switched plans between September 18 and September 28. If your October bank statement shows two debits labeled with your servicer's ACH descriptor on dates between October 1 and October 20, you are in the affected group.

The good news is that the fix is well-practiced by now; dozens of CFPB complaint resolutions this week show a median turnaround of 4 to 5 business days when the borrower calls the servicer the same day the duplicate is spotted. The less-good news is that the window to recover the money closes on a federal 60-day clock that most borrowers do not realize is running. This guide walks the whole process end to end.

How to Confirm a Duplicate (Not Just a Delayed Repost)

Before you call anyone, confirm it is actually a duplicate rather than a reposted debit that had initially failed. The three signatures are specific.

The Three Confirming Signatures

1. Same ACH descriptor on both debits. On your bank statement, both lines read with the servicer's standard ACH naming (for MOHELA: "MOHELA ACH"; for Nelnet: "NELNET ACH PMT"; for Aidvantage: "AIDVANTAGE ED"). A reposted-failure debit sometimes carries a different trailing descriptor code; a duplicate carries the identical descriptor.

2. Both debits posted as "completed" on the servicer portal. Log into your servicer account and open the payment history page. A true duplicate shows two completed postings with consecutive confirmation numbers for the same October statement. A failed-and-reposted debit shows the first posting marked "returned" or "reversed" before the second posting.

3. Both debits are the exact same amount, to the penny. Not a cent different. If the amounts differ, you may instead be looking at an October statement plus a late-posting September payment, which is a different situation and does not qualify as a duplicate.

If all three signatures match, you are looking at a duplicate and the reversal path below applies. If they do not all match, call the servicer anyway but describe the situation accurately; one of the other scripts (misapplied payment, late-posting September bill, failed autopay reposted) may be the right template.

Path 1: Servicer-Initiated Refund (The Fast Path)

The servicer refund is the first path to try. It is the fastest, does not count against your bank's dispute history, and most servicers have been issuing same-day or next-day reversals this week to clear the complaint volume. The call should take 10 to 15 minutes.

Before you dial, have four things in front of you: a screenshot of the duplicate from your bank app showing both dates and amounts, a screenshot of the servicer payment history showing both postings and their confirmation numbers, your loan account number, and a bank statement line showing any overdraft or returned item the duplicate may have caused.

Script That Has Been Working This Week

"My account was debited twice for the October statement, both as autopay, both for the same amount of $[amount], on [date 1] and [date 2]. The two postings in my payment history are confirmation numbers [X] and [Y]. The second debit is a duplicate from a SAVE autopay authorization that did not fully release during the RAP transition. I am requesting a same-day reversal of the second debit, with the refund sent back via the same ACH route to the account it came from. Please confirm the reversal reference number, when the funds will post to my bank, and send me an email confirmation referencing both the duplicate confirmation number and the reversal reference number."

Three things to make sure the agent confirms on the call: (1) the reversal is being processed as a refund to the originating account, not as a credit to the loan balance; (2) your going-forward autopay authorization is the correct one (the new RAP record, not the retired SAVE record); and (3) the second debit's confirmation number is being voided inside the servicer system so it cannot re-queue. All three are routine requests the agents are briefed on this month.

Reversal typically posts to your bank account inside 3 to 5 business days. If it does not post by business day 7, call back once with the original reversal reference number and ask for a status check. If the second call does not produce a posting date within 48 hours, escalate to Path 2 the same day.

Path 2: Bank Regulation E Chargeback (The Backup Path)

Regulation E of the Electronic Fund Transfer Act gives consumers the right to dispute unauthorized electronic debits from a consumer bank account. A duplicate ACH pulled by a servicer whose authorization has lapsed falls squarely inside Reg E's definition of unauthorized. The bank chargeback is slower than a servicer refund (10 to 20 business days versus 3 to 5), but it is a hard legal right rather than a servicer courtesy, and the bank cannot refuse if the dispute is filed inside the 60-day window.

The 60-day clock starts from the date the bank statement showing the duplicate debit is sent to you (not the date you look at it). For most borrowers on monthly bank statements, that gives you roughly 50 to 55 days of real-world buffer. For borrowers on paperless banking with daily statement generation, the clock is effectively running from the debit date itself. Treat 60 days from the duplicate debit date as the practical outer limit.

To file the chargeback: contact your bank's electronic funds dispute line (usually a sub-menu of the main customer service line), reference the specific duplicate transaction by date and amount, request the dispute be filed as an unauthorized ACH debit under Regulation E, and ask for the dispute reference number and expected resolution date in writing. The bank is required to provisionally credit the disputed amount back to your account within 10 business days while the investigation runs; the credit becomes permanent once the bank confirms the duplicate.

One important interaction: if the servicer later issues its own refund for the same duplicate after your bank chargeback has already provisionally credited your account, call the bank the same day you see the servicer refund post and let them know. The bank will typically close the dispute as resolved and no further action is required; letting the duplicate credit sit uncommunicated can trigger a second provisional reversal later.

The "Apply It Forward" Trap: Why Not to Keep the Duplicate on the Loan

A tempting shortcut when you spot the duplicate is to just leave it on the loan as a pre-payment and skip the reversal entirely. For borrowers on the standard 10-year or Tiered Standard plans, this is sometimes fine. For borrowers on RAP (which is most of the SAVE transition cohort), it is almost always the wrong call, and the reason is specific to the RAP interest waiver and 50% principal match.

RAP's interest waiver covers any interest that accrues above the billed monthly payment, as long as the account is in "paid-current" status. The 50% principal match deposits an additional 50 cents on every dollar of billed principal, up to a cap, also conditional on current status. Both benefits are forfeited for the following month when the account enters "paid-ahead" status, which is the default classification when a servicer receives more than the billed amount in a single cycle.

The math for a $10 RAP minimum borrower: forfeiting one month of the interest waiver on, say, a $45,000 balance accruing at 6.5% means losing roughly $244 of interest coverage for that month. Compared to keeping an extra $10 duplicate as a pre-payment, the trade is $234 negative. The gap gets bigger at larger balances. The guide on the RAP pay-ahead status trap walks through the mechanics in detail.

The practical rule for October 2026 duplicates: if you are on RAP, reverse the duplicate rather than apply it forward. The reversal preserves the interest waiver and principal match for November. If you are on IBR, PAYE, or a Standard plan, applying it forward is less costly and reversal is still cleaner but no longer mandatory.

A Worked Example: Priya, Aidvantage, Two $212 Debits

Priya was in SAVE forbearance from April 2024 through September 2026, serviced by Aidvantage. Her account converted to RAP on September 22. Her first RAP statement arrived with a $212 billed amount and an October 12 due date. She had autopay enabled under SAVE and the Aidvantage portal confirmed the new RAP autopay was active for October.

On October 12, $212 debited her checking account. On October 14, $212 debited again. The two ACH descriptors were identical ("AIDVANTAGE ED") and both postings appeared on her Aidvantage payment history with consecutive confirmation numbers. The duplicate caused a $4 overdraft on her account because her November rent check posted the same morning as the second debit.

She called Aidvantage on October 14 with screenshots of both the bank app and the payment history page. The agent confirmed the second debit was tied to the retired SAVE autopay record and processed a same-day reversal with a reference number. The $212 refund posted to her checking account on October 17, three business days later. She emailed her bank's customer service showing the Aidvantage email confirmation, and her bank waived the overdraft fee as a courtesy under its "servicer error" policy. Total time spent: about 25 minutes across two calls and one email.

The piece most borrowers miss is step three: Priya also confirmed with Aidvantage that the retired SAVE autopay record had been voided inside the servicer system, so a November duplicate was not possible. The agent sent a secure message confirming the authorization queue showed only one active record. If you reverse an October duplicate but do not confirm the retired authorization has been voided, a November duplicate becomes significantly more likely.

What If the Duplicate Caused a Cascade (Overdraft, Returned Items, Reported Late)?

For borrowers who were running thin balances before the duplicate hit, the knock-on costs can be larger than the duplicate itself. Overdraft fees average $35 per occurrence; a single returned ACH on rent or a utility can trigger a $30 to $50 late fee from the recipient and, if the recipient reports to credit bureaus, a 30-day late entry on an unrelated account. Here is how to unwind each.

Overdraft fees. Call your bank the same day the fee posts. Explain the duplicate was a servicer error, provide the servicer's reversal reference number if you have it, and request the fee be waived as a one-time courtesy. Approval rates in 2026 are running near 70 percent for first-time waiver requests tied to documented servicer errors.

Returned ACH on rent, utility, or credit card. Call the recipient the same day you learn of the return. Pay the amount by the fastest route available (same-day ACH, debit card, cash app). Document the duplicate debit as the cause and request the late fee be waived. For credit card issuers, explicitly ask that the account not be reported late to the bureaus, which the issuer can suppress if the payment is made within the current statement cycle.

Credit bureau report of an unrelated late. If a knock-on late payment has already furnished to the bureaus (unlikely in the first 60 days), file a goodwill adjustment letter with the reporting creditor citing the documented servicer error. See our October 2026 credit reporting restart guide for the goodwill letter template, which translates directly to any creditor's dispute process.

Preventing a November Duplicate

The October duplicates are concentrated in a specific transition cohort, but a November repeat is possible for any borrower whose October reversal did not also void the retired SAVE authorization. Three preventive checks to run inside the next 14 days.

Check 1: Confirm only one active autopay authorization. Log into your servicer portal, open the autopay settings page, and confirm there is exactly one active authorization on file (the RAP one) with the correct bank account and the next debit date pointing to your November due date. If two authorizations appear, open a secure message to the servicer requesting the SAVE one be voided and ask for written confirmation.

Check 2: Verify your November billed amount matches your RAP calculation. Run your current income through the RAP Calculator and confirm the servicer-billed amount matches to within a few dollars. A mismatch can indicate a billing error separate from the duplicate issue, and the two problems compound.

Check 3: Confirm your plan is correct. Some SAVE borrowers were auto-moved to RAP, some to IBR, and a very small number to the Tiered Standard plan by servicer error. If you are not in the plan you expected, the Plan Comparison tool runs the numbers side by side and the SAVE Transition Guide walks through requesting a plan correction before November's cycle closes.

This Week's Action List

1. Open your bank app and your servicer portal side by side. Look for two matching ACH debits in October from the servicer. If you find them, screenshot both views immediately.

2. Call the servicer the same day. Use the script above. Request a same-day reversal with a reference number sent in writing. Confirm the retired SAVE authorization has been voided inside the system.

3. If the reversal does not post inside 5 business days, escalate to a Regulation E chargeback with your bank. Reference the duplicate transaction by date and amount, cite the 60-day Reg E window, and request provisional credit inside 10 business days.

4. Pursue any overdraft or returned-item fees caused by the duplicate. Each one is a separate call with the fee-charging party; document the servicer error as the cause and request waivers.

5. If you are on RAP, verify your plan status is still "paid current" (not "paid ahead") after the reversal posts. That is the condition for the interest waiver and 50% principal match. Our guide to the interest waiver and principal match covers how to verify the status in the portal.

6. If PSLF is in your plan, confirm the October payment counts. A reversed duplicate does not change the fact that the first debit was a qualifying payment under PSLF rules. Pull your tally from the PSLF Tracker after the reversal clears to confirm the count did not shift.

Bottom Line

A duplicate ACH debit from a federal student loan servicer in October 2026 is almost always a residual SAVE autopay authorization that did not fully retire during the RAP transition. The fix is well-understood by the servicers at this point and the median turnaround for a reversal is 4 to 5 business days when the request is made the same day the duplicate is spotted. The 60-day Regulation E window is the hard deadline for the backup bank chargeback path; the servicer path has no formal deadline but gets slower after week 2. The most expensive mistake is to leave the duplicate on the loan as a pre-payment on RAP, because the resulting "paid-ahead" status forfeits the following month's interest waiver and principal match, which for most borrowers is worth 20 to 30 times the duplicate itself. The second-most-expensive mistake is not confirming on the reversal call that the retired SAVE authorization has been voided, which leaves the door open for a November repeat.

If you want to double-check that your correct RAP payment amount is being billed in the first place (so you can tell whether a debit was valid before the duplicate even entered the picture), run the RAP Calculator with your current income and family size and compare to your October statement. If the two do not match within a few dollars, that is a separate conversation with the servicer worth having before the November cycle closes.
